**Thandi:** Class questions before we wrap up! The loudest one first: the problem contains 110 newtons of pulling in total, so how can the answer possibly be only 30 newtons? What happened to the other 80?

**Tutor:** Nothing disappeared — 80 newtons of effort is locked in a stalemate. Picture the tug-of-war again: one team pulls east with 40 newtons, the other pulls west with 70. The eastward 40 is fully matched by 40 of the westward newtons, and that matched pair — 80 newtons of genuine effort — produces exactly zero motion, because it is spent in cancellation. The unmatched 30 westward newtons are the only ones the crate ever feels, and that leftover is what we call the resultant: the single force whose effect equals the whole contest combined. So the resultant is never "all the force in the problem"; it is what survives after the opposition is settled. If the two forces had pointed the same way, they would be teammates, and 110 newtons would be exactly right — magnitudes add only when directions agree. Notice the bookkeeping closes perfectly: 40 matched, 30 unmatched, and 70 minus 40 is 30 newtons west. All the effort is real; the net force is what remains after the argument.

***

**Thandi:** Next one: why is distance a scalar but displacement a vector? Half the class says both words just mean "how far you went".

**Tutor:** The two words ask different questions, and everyday speech blurs them. Distance asks "how much ground was covered?" — every metre of the path counts, however it twisted, and no direction is involved, so it is a scalar. Displacement asks "where did you end up relative to where you began, and in which direction?" — it consults only the start point and the end point, joined by a straight line with a direction, so it is a vector. Test it on one full jog around a 600-metre block: the distance is 600 metres, every one honestly covered, but the displacement is zero, because the gate you finished at is the gate you left. Real effort, zero net change of position. And since speed is distance over time while velocity is displacement over time, the split cascades: a 150-second loop gives an average speed of 600 divided by 150, which is 4 metres per second, but an average velocity of 0 divided by 150, which is zero. Both answers are correct about the same jog. The skill is reading which of the two questions a problem is actually asking.

***

**Thandi:** This one worried a few people: does the answer change if I pick west as my positive direction instead of east? It feels like it should.

**Tutor:** It cannot change, and proving that to yourself once is worth an hour of revision. The sign convention is nothing but a dictionary between directions and signs; any consistent dictionary tells the same physical story. Run our problem with west positive. The 70-newton westward force is now plus 70; the 40-newton eastward force is now minus 40. Add: plus 70 plus minus 40 gives plus 30. Positive means west in this dictionary, so the resultant is 30 newtons west — identical magnitude, identical direction, different bookkeeping on the way there. What genuinely wrecks solutions is switching dictionaries midstream: east positive in line one, west positive in line three, and every sign after that is meaningless, which makes method marks impossible to award. So write your convention as the first line of working, treat it as law until the final line, and then translate the closing sign into a word — markers want "west", not "minus".

***

**Thandi:** Last one, a double: does a minus sign mean a weaker force? And why does everyone keep calling energy a vector?

**Tutor:** The minus sign first. In one dimension, a sign is purely a direction label — it says nothing about strength. Minus 30 newtons and plus 30 newtons are equally strong pushes aimed opposite ways. A magnitude is always reported as a positive number with its unit, and the direction travels separately in words: with east positive, minus 30 newtons is reported as 30 newtons west. Writing a minus sign inside a final magnitude will cost you credit. Now energy. It gets misfiled as a vector because the word sounds dynamic and because joules appear in problems crowded with forces and velocities. Ignore the atmosphere and run the test: attach a direction and listen. "Six hundred joules of energy, southward" — the sentence collapses, because a charged battery holds its joules identically whichever way you turn it. No direction is needed, so energy is a scalar. The same test cleanly separates the last pair too: 80 kilometres per hour on its own is a speed, a scalar; 80 kilometres per hour due south is a velocity, and the south is not optional.
