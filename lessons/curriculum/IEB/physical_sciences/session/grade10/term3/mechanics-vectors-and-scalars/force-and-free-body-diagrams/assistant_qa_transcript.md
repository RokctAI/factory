**Thandi:** Class questions before we close! Here is the big one: if the forces on the box all balance, why doesn't the box just stand still? Balanced sounds like stuck.

**Tutor:** That confusion is ancient and honourable — it took physics centuries to see through it. The instinct says motion needs a constant cause, that a box left unpushed leaks speed until it stops. What actually leaks the speed away is friction, and rough floors offer plenty. Newton's first law states the repaired rule: an object continues doing whatever it is already doing — resting, or moving straight at a steady pace — until a net force interferes. Force is not fuel for motion; force is the cause of CHANGES in motion. So balanced forces never mean "stop"; they mean "no change". A parked box under balanced forces stays parked. A box already gliding at one metre per second under balanced forces keeps gliding at one metre per second. Our strap is not feeding the motion — it is precisely neutralising the friction that would otherwise eat it. Release the strap and the balance breaks: friction rules alone, the box slows and halts. That is why "constant velocity" and "zero net force" are two spellings of the same fact.

***

**Thandi:** Next: a few people feel cheated that the normal force is not equal to the weight. Every earlier example had them equal. What changed?

**Tutor:** What changed is that a third player entered the vertical game. The normal force was never the weight's twin — it is the surface's response, exactly as large as needed to stop the object sinking in, and not a newton more. Read our vertical balance as a sentence: the floor's upward push plus the strap's upward share together equal the weight of 78,4 newtons. Rearranged: the floor's push equals 78,4 newtons minus the strap's lift. Give it numbers. Suppose the strap pulls with 30 newtons at 25 degrees. The sine of 25 degrees is about 0,42, so the lift is roughly 12,7 newtons — and the floor then supplies only about 65,7 newtons, not 78,4. Reverse the geometry and the arithmetic reverses: push the box along while angling DOWN and your push's vertical part adds to the weight, driving the normal force above 78,4 newtons. So "normal force equals weight" is not a law; it is the lucky special case where nothing else acts vertically. Read the up-and-down balance for the actual situation, every time.

***

**Thandi:** Then this one: how can one strap count as two forces? There is one strap and one pull, so where does the second number come from?

**Tutor:** Nothing is duplicated — one force is holding down two jobs, and components are the payslips that record each job separately. Pull a garden cart by its raised handle: a single effort rolls it forward AND takes some weight off the wheels. Physics logs the rolling job as the horizontal component and the lifting job as the vertical component. For our 30-newton pull at 25 degrees, the horizontal component is 30 times the cosine of 25 degrees — about 27,2 newtons — and the vertical component is 30 times the sine of 25 degrees — about 12,7 newtons. Notice 27,2 plus 12,7 makes 39,9, not 30, and that is no mistake: components live along perpendicular directions and are not slices of one pie. The reward for splitting is that each direction can be balanced on its own: sideways, the 27,2 newtons must equal the friction; vertically, the 12,7 newtons of lift means the floor pushes with less. One strap, one pull, two ledgers.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, from someone who lost a mark on it once: is friction always drawn backwards? They drew it backwards on a ramp question and it was wrong.

**Tutor:** Friction owns no compass direction — it owns a grudge. It always opposes the relative sliding between the two surfaces, or the sliding that is trying to begin. On our flat floor the box slides forward, so friction points backward — but the "backward" is a conclusion, not a rule. Change the scene and the conclusion changes. A crate slipping DOWN a ramp has friction pointing UP the slope, because up-slope opposes that slide. A crate parked motionless on the same ramp still has friction up the slope — static friction, resisting the slide that gravity is trying to start. And the famous curveball: a toolbox on a flatbed truck that pulls away from a stop is carried along BY friction acting FORWARD on the toolbox, because without it the box would slide backwards across the bed. So never memorise a direction. Ask which way this surface slides, or tries to slide, against the other — then aim friction dead against that.
