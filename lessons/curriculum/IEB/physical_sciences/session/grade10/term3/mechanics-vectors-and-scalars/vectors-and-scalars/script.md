# Part 1 — Expert

Every branch of mechanics you will ever study rests on one early decision: knowing which quantities carry a direction and which do not, and then using the correct arithmetic for each kind. Two tasks shape this session — classify displacement, speed, force, energy and velocity as vectors or scalars, and then find the resultant of two forces, 40 newtons east and 70 newtons west, pulling on the same trolley. The questions look small; the idea underneath them runs through every mechanics and electricity topic between now and the end of Grade 12.

## Subtopic: Two Kinds of Quantities

Physics describes the world with measured quantities, and every one of them belongs to one of exactly two families.

A scalar is a quantity that is completely described by a magnitude — a number with its unit. Tell me the kettle has boiled at 100 degrees Celsius and nothing is missing from that sentence; asking "100 degrees towards where?" would be nonsense. Mass, time, temperature, distance, speed and energy all behave this way: number plus unit, description finished.

A vector needs more. It is only fully described when you give BOTH a magnitude AND a direction. Say "a force of 40 newtons acts on the trolley" and the description is unfinished — a physicist immediately wants to know which way. A 40-newton pull towards the door and a 40-newton pull away from it are completely different events sharing one number. Force, displacement, velocity and acceleration are all like this: leave out the direction and you have said only half of what matters.

Here is the test you will use for the rest of your physics career: ask whether a direction is NEEDED to complete the meaning. If the sentence is broken without one, the quantity is a vector. If a direction adds nothing, it is a scalar.

Why does the subject insist on the split? Because the two families obey different rules of arithmetic. Scalars add like ordinary numbers: 4 kilograms plus 3 kilograms is 7 kilograms, every single time. Vectors do not promise that. Forty newtons combined with seventy newtons might strengthen, might weaken, might vanish entirely — it depends on the directions involved. Treating vector quantities with scalar arithmetic is the most stubborn error in early mechanics, and this lesson is where we dig it out.

This section's questions are with you now. They test the two definitions — magnitude alone against magnitude with direction — and the reason the split matters: the two families add differently. Use the direction test on each one rather than reciting a memorised list.

## Subtopic: Classifying the Five — and the Famous Pairs

Now the classification task, one quantity at a time, each with its reason attached — because the reason earns the credit, not the guess.

Displacement: the straight-line change in position, from a starting point to an end point. "The drone is 2 kilometres from the depot" is incomplete — 2 kilometres towards where? Direction required: VECTOR.

Speed: the rate at which distance is covered. A taxi's speedometer shows 80 kilometres per hour whether the taxi is heading to town or coming home. Direction adds nothing: SCALAR.

Force: a push or a pull. Pushing a gate open and pulling it shut can use the same strength and produce opposite results. Direction essential: VECTOR.

Energy: the capacity to do work, measured in joules. A phone battery holding 40 000 joules does not hold them "northwards". SCALAR.

Velocity: the rate of change of position — speed with its direction stitched on. Eighty kilometres per hour DUE SOUTH. VECTOR.

Hidden in that list are the two famous pairs that assessment writers return to year after year. Distance and displacement are the position twins. Jog once around a 600-metre loop that ends at your own front gate: your distance is 600 metres, because every metre of path counts; your displacement is ZERO, because displacement compares only where you finished with where you began, and those are the same spot. Speed and velocity split identically. Suppose the loop took 150 seconds. Average speed is distance over time: 600 metres divided by 150 seconds gives 4 metres per second. Average velocity is displacement over time: 0 divided by 150 is zero. Two truthful answers about one jog, because the scalar reports how much moving occurred while the vector reports what the moving achieved.

Take this section's questions now. They demand classification WITH reasons, and they revisit the famous pairs on that same 600-metre loop — distance against displacement, average speed against average velocity. Write the reason every time; that is where the credit lives.

## Subtopic: The Resultant — 40 N East Meets 70 N West

Now the forces problem. Two ropes pull one trolley: 40 newtons east and 70 newtons west. Find the resultant.

First, the vocabulary. The resultant is the SINGLE vector whose effect equals the combined effect of all the original vectors acting at once. Two ropes are pulling; the resultant is the one imaginary rope that could replace both and leave the trolley none the wiser.

One-dimensional vectors are added using a sign convention, the device that converts directions into algebra. Choose a positive direction, announce it, and never abandon it mid-solution. Choose east as positive. Then 40 newtons east becomes plus 40 newtons, and 70 newtons west — the opposite way — becomes MINUS 70 newtons. Directions have turned into signs, and the physics has become arithmetic.

Add: plus 40 newtons plus negative 70 newtons equals negative 30 newtons.

Now translate back from algebra into the world. Negative, under our chosen convention, means westward. The resultant is 30 newtons WEST.

Read what physically happened: the forces opposed each other. Forty eastward newtons neutralised forty of the seventy westward newtons, and the thirty westward newtons left standing are all the trolley ever feels. That is why the reflex answer of 110 newtons is so badly wrong — it adds the two efforts as if they cooperated, when in truth they spent most of their strength cancelling each other.

State the final answer in full, magnitude AND direction: 30 newtons west. A vector answer missing its direction is half an answer, and marking guidelines penalise it every time. Then run the two sanity checks. The resultant must point the way of the LARGER force — west, correct. Its magnitude must equal the difference of the opposing magnitudes — 70 newtons minus 40 newtons is 30 newtons, correct.

The questions for this section are in front of you. Watch for three habits as you answer: the sign convention declared before any number is touched, the negative result translated back into a direction in words, and both sanity checks applied — resultant with the larger force, magnitude equal to the difference.

## Subtopic: The Toolkit, the Special Cases, and the Road to Matric

Turn the worked example into a permanent four-step method. Step one: declare a positive direction — any choice is legal; loyalty to the choice is what counts. Step two: rewrite every vector as a signed number. Step three: add the signed numbers algebraically. Step four: translate the final sign back into a direction and state the answer as magnitude with direction. Those four steps handle two forces, five forces, or a dozen forces on a line without modification.

Learn the special cases as landmarks. Same direction: 40 newtons east with 70 newtons east gives 110 newtons east — when directions agree, magnitudes simply add, and this is the ONLY situation where the scalar reflex lands on the right number. Opposite directions: subtract, and the resultant sides with the larger force — today's problem. Equal and opposite: 70 newtons east against 70 newtons west gives a resultant of ZERO; the trolley behaves exactly as if nobody touched it. Hold on to that zero case — it is the equilibrium condition sitting at the centre of Newton's first law, waiting for you in the free-body diagram work ahead and throughout Grade 11.

Now the error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: adding magnitudes while ignoring directions — the 110-newton disaster. Exhibit two: dropping the direction from the final line — 30 newtons is incomplete; 30 newtons west is physics. Exhibit three: swapping sign conventions halfway through — east positive in the first line, west positive by the fourth; declare once and stay loyal. Exhibit four: classifying by vibe instead of by test — "energy feels forceful, so it must be a vector"; feelings are not the test; NEEDING a direction is the test.

Where does this carry you? Everywhere the subject goes. Grade 11 opens with vectors in two dimensions, where forces at angles are broken into components. Newton's laws are statements about vectors; momentum in Grade 12 is a vector; electric fields point, so they are vectors too. Mechanics and electromagnetism dominate the physics you will write in your final exams, and every page of it speaks today's language. Classify with the direction test; add with a sign convention; answer with magnitude and direction. Three habits, and years of physics stand on them.

The final questions of this part are ready for you: the four-step method, the three special cases, and the four exhibits in the error museum. Give every answer its direction — exactly as a marker will expect.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same two families of quantities, but now from the street, the shop and the sports field, where directions are worth arguing about.

## Subtopic: Does It Need a "Which Way"?

A friend texts: "I'm four kilometres away." What do you type back before anything else? Which way — four kilometres in which direction? Now the shopkeeper says the airtime voucher costs thirty rand. Nobody on Earth replies, "thirty rand in which direction?" That one difference is the whole lesson, and you have been using it since before you could spell physics.

Some measurements are complete the instant you say the number and the unit. Thirty rand. Three litres of milk. Twenty-six degrees on a humid Durban morning. A fifty-minute football half. Number, unit, finished — physics calls these scalars.

Other measurements dangle unfinished until a direction arrives. A shove on a stuck gate: 40 newtons — inwards or outwards? Same number, opposite outcomes. A journey: two kilometres — towards the taxi rank or away from it? A speed on a road sign stands happily alone; a velocity is a speed that has confessed where it is going. These are vectors, and the direction is not decoration — it is half the meaning.

So skip the memorised list and run the sentence test out loud. Bolt a direction onto the quantity and listen to whether the sentence survives. "A force of forty newtons westward" — survives, and needed the direction. "Six hundred joules of energy southward"? Collapses instantly; a battery does not aim anywhere. Energy is a scalar no matter how mighty the word sounds — and "sounds mighty" was never the test. Needing a which-way is the test.

Your questions for this section are up now, and they are the gentlest in the lesson. Say the sentence test out loud for each quantity: if the direction is needed, vector; if the sentence falls over, scalar. You owned this instinct long before today — now it has a name.

## Subtopic: The Lap Around the Block

Here is the pair that trips more learners than any other, and one easy jog untangles it permanently.

You leave your front gate, jog once around the block, and stop at the exact same gate. How far did you jog? If the block measures 600 metres around, you jogged 600 metres — your legs felt every one, and that total is the distance: the whole path added up, no direction attached. Scalar.

Different question about the same jog: where are you now, compared with where you started? Precisely where you started. So your displacement is zero metres, because displacement compares only the finish with the start, in a straight line, direction included. Whatever wandering happened in between is not its concern. Vector.

Two fair questions, two different answers, and no contradiction — they were never asking the same thing. Distance asks how much moving happened. Displacement asks what the moving accomplished. The classic assessment version is a full loop of a running track: big distance, zero displacement.

Speed and velocity inherit the split, and you can now predict it before I say it. Let the lap take 150 seconds. Average speed is distance divided by time: 600 metres over 150 seconds is 4 metres per second — you were genuinely moving, and the number honours that. Average velocity is displacement divided by time: zero over 150 is zero metres per second. You moved plenty and got nowhere, and both statements are true. Now jog those 600 metres dead straight along the main road instead: distance still 600 metres, but displacement becomes 600 metres south, and the average velocity becomes 4 metres per second south.

Here come your questions for this section, and you are ready. Carry the jog with you: for each question, decide which thing is really being asked — how much moving happened, or what the moving achieved. That one habit closes the distance-displacement trap for good.

## Subtopic: Two Teams on One Rope

Last piece — the one that looks like arithmetic gone mad until you watch it happen in the real world.

Forty newtons drag a crate east while seventy newtons drag it west. Total force? Instinct yells one hundred and ten, and instinct is wrong, because these forces are not on the same side — they are opponents. Picture the tug-of-war at a sports day. The smaller team pulls with forty newtons; the bigger team answers with seventy. Forty of the bigger team's newtons are completely tied up cancelling the smaller team. Only thirty newtons remain free to actually move anything. The rope inches towards the stronger side exactly as if a single person were pulling it with thirty newtons. That survivor is the resultant: the one pull that replaces the entire contest.

To capture this on paper, turn directions into signs. Pick a direction, name it positive, and say so before any arithmetic — east, let us say. Forty newtons east becomes plus forty; seventy newtons west becomes minus seventy, because it points the opposite way. Add like ordinary numbers: plus forty plus minus seventy is minus thirty. Now translate the sign back into words: minus meant west, so the resultant is thirty newtons west.

Two head-checks before you write anything down. The winning direction should belong to the bigger force — west, correct. The size should be the gap between them — seventy minus forty is thirty, correct. If the forces had pulled the same way, forty east and seventy east, they would be teammates and one hundred and ten newtons east would be exactly right — the single case where instinct survives. And if they had been equal and opposite, seventy east against seventy west, the resultant is zero: the crate feels nothing while both teams sweat.

One closing habit, worth credit in every vector question you will ever answer: finish the sentence. "Thirty newtons" is half an answer. "Thirty newtons west" is physics. Magnitude and direction, every single time.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now, and you have earned them. Declare your positive direction before touching the numbers, hold it to the last line, and send every answer out with its direction attached. Keep that up and you already own the habit that carries you through Grade 11 forces and Grade 12 momentum.
