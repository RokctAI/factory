# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds kinetic energy — the energy an object carries because it is moving — and the formula that prices it: E k equals a half m v squared. One problem carries everything: an 800 kilogram bakkie travels at 15 metres per second; calculate its kinetic energy, and then determine what happens to that energy when the driver doubles the speed to 30 metres per second. The second answer is the most important road-safety fact in the entire physics course, and the little square in the formula is single-handedly responsible for it.

## Subtopic: Energy Carried by Motion

Kinetic energy is the energy an object possesses because of its MOTION. Anything moving can do work on whatever it meets — a swinging hammer drives a nail, moving water spins a turbine, moving air bends a bluegum tree. Bring the moving thing to rest and its energy must be delivered somewhere: into the nail, the turbine, the crumpled metal. That capacity to do work by moving is kinetic energy.

The formula: E k equals one half, times the mass, times the velocity squared — a half m v squared. Mass in kilograms, velocity in metres per second, answer in joules — the identical joule that priced yesterday's potential energy, because energy is one currency spread across many accounts.

Study the formula's two dials. Mass appears once, plainly: double the mass, double the energy — a fully loaded bakkie carries twice the kinetic energy of the empty one at the same speed. Velocity appears SQUARED: double the speed and the energy multiplies by four; triple it, by nine. The two dials are nothing alike, and the squared one is the dial with consequences.

One more property before any numbers: kinetic energy is a scalar. The velocity gets squared, and squaring erases direction — a bakkie doing 15 metres per second north and one doing 15 south carry identical kinetic energy. No direction ever attaches to an energy answer, and E k is never negative: mass is positive and a square cannot dip below zero. Zero — anything at rest — is the floor.

The questions for this section are with you now. They examine the definition, the two dials of the formula — one plain, one squared — the scalar nature of E k, and why kinetic energy can never be negative.

## Subtopic: Pricing the Motion — a Bakkie at Fifteen

Now the problem. Data first: mass 800 kilograms — a light bakkie. Velocity: 15 metres per second — and feel that speed properly: 15 times 3,6 is 54 kilometres per hour, an ordinary suburban main road.

Substitute into E k equals a half m v squared. The square first, always: 15 squared is 225. Then assemble: a half times 800 is 400, and 400 times 225 — 90 000 joules. Ninety thousand joules — 90 kilojoules — riding along in an ordinary bakkie at an ordinary speed.

Give the number a body: 90 000 joules is yesterday's 4 kilogram paint tin hoisted more than two kilometres into the sky — far above any crane. Every joule of it must be removed by the brakes to stop the bakkie, converted into heat in the discs. Brakes are energy furnaces; that is their entire job.

Now watch the commonest error die. A learner who forgets the square computes a half times 800 times a plain 15 — 6 000 joules, fifteen times too small. The defence is mechanical: write the square as its own line — v squared equals 225 — before touching anything else. A square handled first is never forgotten.

Units close the loop: kilograms times metres squared per second squared is exactly the joule. And when a speed arrives in kilometres per hour, divide by 3,6 BEFORE squaring — 54 kilometres per hour is 15 metres per second; square the wrong unit and the error squares itself, a factor of about thirteen.

Take this section's questions now. They walk the calculation: the 225 of the square, the 90 000 joules, the tin-at-altitude comparison, and the fifteen-fold penalty for a forgotten square.

## Subtopic: The Square — Why Doubling Speed Quadruples the Danger

Now the driver doubles the speed: 30 metres per second — 108 kilometres per hour. Recompute: 30 squared is 900; a half times 800 times 900 — 360 000 joules.

Compare: 360 000 against 90 000. Double the speed, FOUR times the energy. Not double — four times, because velocity enters squared and 2 squared is 4. Triple the speed and the factor is 9. The link between speed and energy is no straight line; it is a curve that steepens viciously.

Translate the mathematics onto the road. All 360 000 joules must be dissipated to stop — by brakes in the good story, by folding metal in the bad one. Four times the energy means, for the same braking force, roughly four times the braking distance — the stopping gap grows with the SQUARE of the speed, which is why a following distance that feels generous at 60 kilometres per hour becomes lethal at 120. In a collision, four times the energy is four times the destruction to be absorbed: a crash at 80 is not twice a crash at 40 — it is four of them at once. Speed limits are not linear caution; they are quadratic arithmetic.

The square also produces an asymmetry worth pinning up. For our 800 kilogram bakkie, run three speedometer readings through the formula, dividing by 3,6 first. At 60 kilometres per hour — 16,7 metres per second — the energy is about 112 000 joules. At 100 — 27,8 metres per second — about 309 000. At 120 — 33,3 metres per second — about 444 000. The final 20 kilometres per hour of speed cost about 135 000 joules; the entire climb from rest to 60 cost only 112 000. The top of the speed range is where the energy lives.

This section's questions are in front of you. They examine the four-times result, the square behind it, the braking-distance consequence, and the top-heavy way energy piles up at high speed.

## Subtopic: The Full Method, the Classic Traps, and Where This Leads

The method, four steps. Step one: list mass and velocity, converting as you list — grams to kilograms, kilometres per hour to metres per second by dividing by 3,6. Step two: square the velocity on its own written line. Step three: assemble a half times m times v squared, carrying units. Step four: sense-check — the answer must be positive, carry no direction, and sit at a believable scale: tens or hundreds of joules for balls and bricks, tens or hundreds of thousands for vehicles.

The traps. Trap one: the forgotten square — the fifteen-fold error you have already watched die; keep it dead with the separate line. Trap two: squaring the wrong unit — convert to metres per second first, then square; 54 squared and 15 squared differ by a factor of about thirteen. Trap three: dropping the half — E k is HALF m v squared; the factor comes from building speed gradually from rest, and omitting it doubles every answer. Trap four: attaching a direction or a minus sign — kinetic energy is a scalar with its floor at zero; an object moving in the negative direction still holds positive E k, because the square destroyed the sign. Trap five: comparing energies by comparing speeds linearly — square first, then compare.

Where this leads: next, conservation of mechanical energy merges E p and E k into one budget — height converting to speed and back, the total constant while only gravity acts. That principle demolishes falling, swinging and rolling problems in three lines each. Grade 12 sharpens the story into the work-energy theorem and collision analysis, where kinetic energy decides what survives. And every road-safety statistic you will ever read is today's square, dressed in data.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the four steps, the five traps — the square, the units, the half — and the quadratic heart of road safety. Answer them as method, not memory.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same energy again — from the delivery route and the toll road. Motion as money, and the square as the surcharge nobody sees coming.

## Subtopic: The Moving Wallet

Yesterday's picture was a bank account in the sky: lift something, and its height holds the deposit. Today's picture is a wallet that exists only while you move: kinetic energy, the cash an object carries because it is in motion.

Everything moving carries this wallet. A rolling cricket ball carries small change — enough to rattle the stumps. A delivery bakkie at highway speed carries a fortune. And the wallet's entire meaning shows at the moment of stopping: the cash MUST be spent, every cent, on whatever does the stopping. Caught softly by giving hands, it is spent gently across a metre of give. Stopped by a wall, it is spent instantly on bending metal. The wallet never simply vanishes; stopping is spending.

The price list is E k equals a half m v squared — half the mass, times the speed times itself. Two dials set the balance. The mass dial is honest and dull: double the load, double the cash — a fully loaded bakkie carries twice the empty one's energy at the same speed. The speed dial is the fascinating one, because the speed multiplies by ITSELF: twice the speed is not twice the cash — it is four times. Hold that; the whole second half of this lesson lives inside it.

One friendly footnote: the wallet is colour-blind to direction. North at 15 or south at 15, the cash is identical — squaring wipes out the sign, so energy never points and never goes negative. Standing still is simply an empty wallet.

Your questions for this section are ready now. Keep the wallet: moving is carrying cash, stopping is spending all of it, and the two dials — plain mass, squared speed — decide how much is inside.

## Subtopic: Counting the Bakkie's Cash

Count a real wallet. A delivery bakkie — 800 kilograms — cruising at 15 metres per second, which reads 54 kilometres per hour on the speedometer: an ordinary trip across town.

The price list says: square the speed first. Fifteen times fifteen — 225. Then half the mass: half of 800 is 400. Multiply: 400 times 225 — 90 000 joules. Ninety thousand joules of motion-cash in an ordinary bakkie at an ordinary speed.

A number that size needs a picture. Ninety thousand joules is yesterday's 4 kilogram paint tin lifted more than TWO KILOMETRES straight up — higher than any crane on Earth. That is what the brakes must swallow at every stop, turning the whole wallet into heat in the discs. It is also what crumple zones must swallow in an accident — which is why modern vehicles are built to fold at the front like cardboard: folding metal is the least bad way to spend a fortune fast.

And here is the fifteen-times warning, cheap to learn today and ruinous later: skip the square — compute half of 800 times a plain 15 — and you get 6 000 joules, a wallet fifteen times too thin. The habit that saves you is one written line before anything else: v squared equals 225. Square first, on its own line, every time. And if the speedometer handed you kilometres per hour, divide by 3,6 before that line — the square must be taken in metres per second.

The questions for this section are up now. Count carefully: square first as its own step, half the mass, multiply — and give every big answer a picture to check it against.

## Subtopic: The Quadratic Surcharge

Now the lesson inside the lesson. The driver doubles the speed: 30 metres per second — 108 on the speedometer. The wallet: 30 squared is 900; half of 800 is 400; multiply — 360 000 joules.

Double the speed. FOUR times the cash. That is the quadratic surcharge, and it is no accounting quirk — it is the reason speed kills. Every extra bit of pace costs energy at a growing rate, like a toll road where the fee per kilometre keeps climbing the further you go: the step from 100 to 120 kilometres per hour deposits MORE energy than the entire climb from rest to 60. The top of the speed range is where the fortune piles up.

Spend the surcharge on the road and it reads like this. Four times the energy needs roughly four times the stopping distance for the same brakes — the gap that protects you at 60 is nowhere near enough at 120, not by a margin but by a factor. Hit something, and four times the energy is four times the wreckage: a collision at 80 is four collisions at 40 happening simultaneously. Speed limits are drawn from this square, not from timidity — the difference between 60 and 120 in a crash is not twice as bad; it is four times as bad, every time, by arithmetic no argument can soften.

So carry two sentences out of this lesson. Motion is a wallet, and stopping spends every cent — through brakes as heat, or through metal as wreckage. And the wallet grows with the SQUARE of the speed — double the pace, four times the fortune waiting to be spent.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now, and you are ready. Square first, half the mass, multiply — and whenever two speeds are compared, compare their squares, because that is exactly how the road will compare them for you.
