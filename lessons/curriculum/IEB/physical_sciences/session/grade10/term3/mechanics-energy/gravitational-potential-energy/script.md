# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds gravitational potential energy — the energy an object banks purely because of where it sits in a gravitational field — and the formula that prices it: E p equals m g h. One problem carries the hour: a painter hoists a 4 kilogram paint tin from the ground up to a platform 3 metres high; calculate the gravitational potential energy the tin gains, and then the further gain when the tin is raised to a higher platform at 10 metres. Modest numbers — but every idea in the energy chapter is seeded here.

## Subtopic: Energy, the Joule, and Energy Stored by Position

Energy is the capacity to do work — to set things moving, bend them, heat them or break them. Its unit is the joule, symbol J. One joule is roughly the effort of lifting a small apple through one metre; the joule is small change, and real problems trade in hundreds and thousands of them.

Gravitational potential energy is energy stored by POSITION in the gravitational field. To lift an object you must work against gravity's pull, and that work is not lost — it is deposited in the object's raised position, waiting to be repaid the instant the object falls. A paint tin on a high platform is dangerous for exactly this reason; a dam full of high water is valuable for exactly the same one. Stored, patient, recoverable — that is what "potential" means.

The formula prices the storage: E p equals m g h — gravitational potential energy equals mass, times gravitational acceleration, times height. Mass m in kilograms; g at 9,8 metres per second squared near Earth's surface; height h in metres, measured from a reference level you choose. Read the sense inside the multiplication: m times g is the object's weight — the force gravity exerts on it — and weight times the height lifted is the work done against gravity. The formula is nothing mysterious; it is the lifting bill, itemised.

And note what E p is: a scalar. Energy has magnitude and no direction — joules never point. No energy answer ever carries a direction.

The questions for this section are with you now. They examine the joule, the storage-by-position idea, the formula read as weight times height, and the scalar nature of every energy quantity.

## Subtopic: Pricing the Lift — 4 Kilograms to 3 Metres

Now the problem, data first, formally. Mass: 4 kilograms. Gravitational acceleration: 9,8 metres per second squared. Height: 3 metres above the ground — and we choose the ground as the reference level: height zero, energy zero.

Substitute into E p equals m g h: 4 times 9,8 times 3. Compute in stages: 4 times 9,8 is 39,2. Then 39,2 times 3 — 117,6. The tin gains 117,6 joules of gravitational potential energy.

Watch the units earn their keep. Kilograms times metres per second squared gives newtons — that 39,2 is the tin's weight, 39,2 newtons. Newtons times metres gives newton-metres, and a newton-metre is exactly a joule. The units assemble themselves into joules, quietly confirming the substitution was sound.

Now the higher platform at 10 metres. Total energy there: 4 times 9,8 times 10 — 392 joules. But the question wants the FURTHER gain from 3 metres to 10: 392 minus 117,6 — 274,4 joules. Or price only the extra climb: the height difference is 7 metres, so 4 times 9,8 times 7 is 274,4 joules — the same answer by a shorter road. Energy changes care only about height changes; hold that thought, because it is about to become a principle.

Sense-check the sizes: around a hundred joules for a tin lifted to head height — believable. An answer in the hundreds of thousands would be shouting that a unit slipped somewhere.

Take this section's questions now. They walk the calculation: the 117,6 joules, the units assembling into joules through the weight, and the two roads to the 274,4 joule further gain.

## Subtopic: The Reference Level — Where Zero Lives

Here is a subtlety with real marks attached: the height h is measured from a reference level that YOU choose — and the choice changes the numbers without changing the physics.

Take the tin on the 3 metre platform. Measured from the ground: h is 3, E p is 117,6 joules. Now put a second painter in a cellar 2 metres below ground, measuring from there: h is 5 metres, and E p is 4 times 9,8 times 5 — 196 joules. Same tin, same platform, two different energies. Which is right? Both — each is correct relative to its own declared zero.

The resolution: nature only ever cashes in energy DIFFERENCES. When the tin falls from the platform to the ground it releases 117,6 joules — and both bookkeepers agree. The ground observer computes 117,6 minus zero. The cellar observer computes 196 minus 78,4 — because ground level, seen from the cellar, holds 4 times 9,8 times 2, which is 78,4 joules — and lands on the same 117,6. The zero is bookkeeping; the differences are physics.

Practical consequences, three. One: declare the reference level in every solution — usually the ground or the lowest point in the problem, chosen so the heights stay positive and friendly. Two: an object BELOW your reference level carries negative potential energy — a legitimate, meaningful statement that it holds less energy than it would at zero. Three: never mix reference levels within one problem — measuring one height from the ground and another from a platform produces numbers that cannot be compared, and that mixture is the commonest error in this subtopic.

This section's questions are in front of you. They probe the free choice of zero, the 196 joules of the cellar bookkeeper, the agreement on differences, and the one forbidden move — switching levels mid-problem.

## Subtopic: The Full Method, the Classic Traps, and Where This Leads

The method for every potential energy problem, four steps. Step one: list mass, g, and height — converting as you list: grams to kilograms, centimetres to metres. Step two: declare the reference level and measure every height from it. Step three: substitute into E p equals m g h and compute, carrying the units. Step four: sense-check the size — and when the question involves a change, work directly with the height difference.

The traps. Trap one: feeding weight where mass belongs — if a question supplies a weight of 39,2 newtons, the formula's m is 4 kilograms, weight divided by g; multiplying 39,2 by g again inflates the answer nearly tenfold. Trap two: height measured along a slope — h is VERTICAL height only; a wheelbarrow pushed 8 metres up a ramp that rises 2 metres has gained a height of 2, and gravity bills only for the 2. Trap three: unit slips — 750 grams is 0,75 kilograms; 320 centimetres is 3,2 metres. Trap four: pinning a direction onto an energy answer — energy is a scalar, and "117,6 joules upward" is not a thing. Trap five: rounding g to 10 — use 9,8 metres per second squared unless a question explicitly instructs otherwise.

Where this leads: next in the energy chapter, kinetic energy prices motion the way E p prices position — and then conservation of mechanical energy links the two, letting stored E p convert into speed and back, which is how physics predicts a falling object's speed without touching the equations of motion. Grade 12 rebuilds the whole story as the work-energy theorem, the most powerful tool in senior mechanics. Every one of those topics quotes today's formula.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the four steps, the five traps — the weight-versus-mass swap and the slope's vertical height above all — and the road ahead through kinetic energy. Answer them as method, not memory.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same energy again — through a water tower and a savings account, with height as money in the bank.

## Subtopic: The Bank Account in the Sky

Every water tower is a savings account on stilts. The pump spends electricity all night pushing water up into the tank — genuine work, paid in full. But the spending is not waste: it is a DEPOSIT. The water sits up there holding the energy, and when a tap opens in the morning, the height pays it back as rushing pressure. Work in, storage, work out.

That is gravitational potential energy: energy banked by lifting something against gravity. Hoist a paint tin to a platform and the effort of the hoist is deposited in the tin's position. It waits — an hour, a month — and the moment the tin falls, the account pays out in speed. Stored because of where it is: that is the entire idea.

The price of any deposit is the lifting bill: E p equals m g h — mass times gravitational acceleration times height. The bill reads naturally in two halves. Mass times g is the object's WEIGHT — how hard gravity drags it down, in newtons; that is the force you must overcome to lift it. Weight times height is force times the distance lifted — the full cost of carrying it to the top. Heavier object, steeper bill; higher lift, steeper bill; both together, steepest of all.

The currency is the joule — about the effort of lifting an apple through one metre. Ordinary deposits run to hundreds of joules; keep that scale handy for sense-checking.

Your questions for this section are ready now. Hold on to the water tower: lifting is depositing, sitting high is savings, falling is the withdrawal — and the bill for any deposit is weight times height.

## Subtopic: The Paint Tin's Bank Balance

Run the actual problem as a bank statement. The tin: 4 kilograms. Gravity's pull on it: 4 times 9,8 — 39,2 newtons of weight. The hoist: 3 metres straight up to the platform.

The deposit: weight times height — 39,2 times 3 — 117,6 joules. The tin sits on the platform with 117,6 joules in its account, measured from the ground where we zeroed the balance.

Now the painter needs it higher: a platform at 10 metres. Two ways to price the top-up, and they must agree. Road one: full balance up top minus the balance already banked — 4 times 9,8 times 10 is 392 joules, minus the 117,6 — 274,4 joules. Road two: bill only the extra climb — 7 more metres at 39,2 newtons is 39,2 times 7 — 274,4 joules. Same number, and the agreement carries the lesson: gravity charges per metre climbed, so only the CHANGE in height ever matters to a change in energy.

Feel the scale of the danger too — this is why building sites insist on hard hats. Those 392 joules come back out in full if the tin drops from 10 metres: every joule of deposit converts to speed on the way down. Stored energy is real energy; height is a loaded account.

The questions for this section are up now. Work them like statements: weight first, then weight times height for any deposit, and height DIFFERENCE times weight for any top-up. The account arithmetic never changes.

## Subtopic: Choosing Where Zero Is

Last piece — the one that looks like a paradox and turns out to be a freedom. Where exactly does the account read zero?

A bank measures your balance from zero rand, but heights arrive with no built-in zero. The painter measures the platform from the ground and says 3 metres; a colleague in the cellar, 2 metres lower, measures the same platform and says 5. Their energy claims disagree — 117,6 joules against 196 — and both are right, each from its own zero. It is like two people calling the same room "first floor" and "second floor" because one counts from the street and one from the parking level: the room never moved.

Physics escapes the paradox because nature only ever pays out DIFFERENCES. The tin falling from platform to ground releases 117,6 joules — and both bookkeepers calculate exactly that, because their zero-point disagreement subtracts itself away. The zero is your free choice; the payouts are fixed by the fall.

Use the freedom with three habits. Declare your zero in the first line — "reference level: the ground" — the way you declare a positive direction in motion problems. Prefer the LOWEST point in the problem as zero, so every height stays positive and the arithmetic stays friendly. And never switch zeros halfway — one account, one zero, from first line to final answer. An object below your chosen zero simply runs a negative balance — overdrawn, meaningfully so, and the mathematics handles it without complaint.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now, and you are ready. Answer them like an accountant with one iron rule: any zero you like, declared once, used throughout — and let the differences, which are the physics, look after themselves.
