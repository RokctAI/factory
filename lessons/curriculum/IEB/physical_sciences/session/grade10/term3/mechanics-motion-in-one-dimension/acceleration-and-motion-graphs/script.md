# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds acceleration — the rate at which velocity changes — and the three motion graphs that convert any journey into a picture worth marks. One problem drives everything: a bus pulls away from rest and speeds up uniformly to 15 metres per second in 6 seconds, holds 15 metres per second for 30 seconds, then brakes uniformly to rest in 10 seconds. We must find both accelerations, and then use the velocity-time graph to find the total displacement — no equations of motion required, only a gradient and an area.

## Subtopic: Acceleration — the Rate Velocity Changes

Start with the definition spoken as a sentence: acceleration equals the change in velocity divided by the time that change took — a equals delta v over delta t. Delta v itself is final velocity minus initial velocity. The unit: metres per second, per second — written metres per second squared. An acceleration of 2,5 metres per second squared promises that the velocity grows by 2,5 metres per second during every single second.

Work the bus's first phase: from rest — initial velocity zero — up to 15 metres per second, in 6 seconds. Delta v is 15 minus zero — 15 metres per second. Divide by 6 seconds: a equals 2,5 metres per second squared, in the direction of motion. Each second of that phase added the same 2,5 metres per second: zero, 2,5, 5, 7,5, on up to 15. That evenness is exactly what uniform acceleration means.

Phase two, the cruise: velocity pinned at 15. Delta v is zero, so the acceleration is zero. Constant velocity is zero acceleration — the motion continues; the change has stopped.

Phase three, the braking: from 15 metres per second to rest in 10 seconds. Delta v is zero minus 15 — negative 15. Divide by 10: negative 1,5 metres per second squared. That sign is working honestly: with the direction of motion positive, a negative acceleration means velocity is being drained — 1,5 metres per second of speed surrendered every second. Watch the vocabulary: here the negative acceleration means slowing down, which everyday speech calls deceleration — but the sign really belongs to the frame, and later problems will show a negative acceleration speeding an object up in the negative direction. Always read signs against the declared frame.

The questions for this section are with you now. They examine the definition read as a sentence, the 2,5 and the negative 1,5, the zero of the cruise, and what an acceleration number promises about each second of motion.

## Subtopic: The Position-Time Graph — Gradient Is Velocity

Now the pictures. First, the position-time graph — position up the vertical axis, time along the horizontal.

The one reading rule everything hangs on: the GRADIENT of a position-time graph is the velocity. Gradient is rise over run — change in position over change in time — and that ratio is the very definition of velocity. Steep means fast; shallow means slow; flat means parked — a horizontal line is an object standing still while the clock runs.

So the basic shapes each tell a story. A straight sloped line: constant gradient, constant velocity — equal ground in equal seconds. A horizontal line: zero velocity, stationary. A CURVE: a changing gradient, hence changing velocity — acceleration. During the bus's pull-away the position-time graph curves upward and steepens — every second covers more ground than the one before, the gradient climbing from zero towards 15. Through the cruise it runs as a straight sloped line. Under braking it curves once more, bending towards horizontal — the gradient dying to zero as the bus halts.

One further shape finishes the kit: a straight line sloping DOWNWARD. Position shrinking — the object heading back towards, and maybe past, the reference point. Negative gradient, negative velocity. No arrows needed on the page; the slope itself carries the direction.

Take this section's questions now. They test the gradient rule, the four shapes — sloped line, flat line, steepening curve, flattening curve — and the downward slope that quietly announces motion in the negative direction.

## Subtopic: The Velocity-Time Graph — Gradient and Area, Both Paying

The velocity-time graph is the workhorse of Grade 10 motion, because it pays out twice.

First payment: the GRADIENT of a velocity-time graph is the acceleration — change in velocity over change in time, which is the definition itself. Draw the bus's trip: from the origin, a straight line climbing to 15 metres per second at 6 seconds — gradient 15 over 6, which is 2,5: the acceleration we computed. Then a horizontal line at 15 lasting 30 seconds — gradient zero, acceleration zero. Finally a straight line dropping from 15 to zero across 10 seconds — gradient negative 15 over 10, negative 1,5. All three phases read directly off the slopes.

Second payment: the AREA between the graph and the time axis is the displacement. Velocity multiplied by time gives displacement, and area is precisely that multiplication carried out piece by piece. Compute the three pieces. Phase one: a triangle — half base times height — half times 6 seconds times 15 metres per second: 45 metres. Phase two: a rectangle — 30 seconds times 15 metres per second: 450 metres. Phase three: another triangle — half times 10 times 15: 75 metres. Total displacement: 45 plus 450 plus 75 — 570 metres.

Stop and admire what happened: a two-part question — accelerations and displacement — answered entirely by reading one picture. Gradient for acceleration, area for displacement. No equations of motion, nothing memorised beyond the two graph rules. That is why assessors adore velocity-time graphs, and why the sharpest students sketch one even when the question never asked for it.

This section's questions are in front of you. They examine the two payment rules, the three gradients — 2,5, zero, negative 1,5 — the triangle-rectangle-triangle areas, and the 570 metres they add up to.

## Subtopic: Reading All Three Graphs Together, and the Classic Traps

The third graph: acceleration-time. For uniformly accelerated motion it is the plainest picture and the cheapest marks — horizontal steps. Our bus: a flat line at 2,5 for six seconds, a flat line at zero for thirty, a flat line at negative 1,5 for ten. Constant values; nothing to differentiate.

Now see the family as a family. The three graphs are one motion in three costumes, linked by two operations. Gradients travel DOWN the family: the gradient of position-time yields velocity, and the gradient of velocity-time yields acceleration. Areas travel UP: the area under acceleration-time yields the change in velocity, and the area under velocity-time yields the displacement. Questions hand you one graph and demand another — those two links are the entire translation dictionary.

The traps, in the order they harvest marks. Trap one: reading a velocity-time graph as if it were position-time — a rising line means speeding up, not moving away; a falling line means slowing down, not coming home. It is the commonest graph error at this level. Trap two: mixing up curve and slope — a curve on position-time signals acceleration, while a straight sloped line on velocity-time signals acceleration; identical physics draws differently on different axes. Trap three: forgetting that a horizontal velocity-time line at height is steady MOTION, not rest — rest is the line lying ON the time axis. Trap four: taking the area only up to the graph line rather than down to the axis, or dropping one piece of the total. Trap five: gradient units — a velocity-time gradient of 2,5 is 2,5 metres per second squared, never 2,5 metres per second; the axes' units divide.

Where this heads: instantaneous velocity arrives through the tangent to a curved position-time graph — the gradient at one point — in the very next topic. The equations of motion are the velocity-time graph rewritten as algebra: the displacement formulas are literally this triangle and rectangle summed. Grade 11 and Grade 12 read forces off these same pictures through Newton's second law. Learn to read the drawings now, and half of mechanics becomes looking instead of calculating.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the flat steps of acceleration-time, the gradient-down area-up family links, and the five traps — above all the rising-line misreading. Answer them with the pictures in your head.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same trip again — but through a bus windscreen this time, with a needle, a silhouette, and an area that pays out in metres.

## Subtopic: The Speedometer's Diary

Take the seat behind the bus driver and watch nothing but the speedometer needle. Acceleration is simply the needle's diary: how fast is the NEEDLE itself moving?

Pulling out of the terminus, the needle climbs — zero, 2,5, 5, 7,5 — collecting 2,5 metres per second with every passing second. That steady climb is an acceleration of 2,5 metres per second squared. Say the unit the friendly way: two and a half metres per second, per second — every second, another two and a half metres per second. The formula is the diary entry in symbols: acceleration equals the change in velocity divided by the time it took — 15 metres per second gained across 6 seconds is 2,5 per second, per second.

Out on the main road the needle settles on 15. The bus is moving — briskly — but the NEEDLE is still. No change in velocity, no acceleration. Zero acceleration never means parked; it means the speed has stopped changing. Absorb that one sentence properly and it will spare you years of confusion.

Then the traffic light ahead flicks to red and the driver brakes: the needle sinks from 15 to zero across 10 seconds — surrendering 1,5 metres per second each second. Change in velocity: minus 15. Acceleration: minus 1,5 metres per second squared. The minus records only which way the needle travelled — down instead of up. And your body reads all three phases without a formula: pressed back into the seat while the needle climbs, at ease while it rests, tipped forward while it falls. Acceleration is the phase you can feel.

Your questions for this section are ready now. Keep the needle in view: acceleration is about the needle moving, never merely the bus moving — climbing needle positive, resting needle zero, sinking needle negative.

## Subtopic: The Journey as a Picture

Now draw the whole trip as a velocity-time graph — time along the bottom, speedometer reading up the side — and watch the journey turn into a shape.

Phase one: the needle climbed steadily from zero to 15 in 6 seconds. On the paper, a straight ramp rising out of the corner. Phase two: thirty seconds cruising at 15 — a long flat plateau riding high above the axis. Phase three: braking to rest across 10 seconds — a gentler ramp easing back down to the floor. Ramp up, plateau, ramp down: the whole story in one silhouette, like a flat-topped hill against the sky.

Reading the silhouette is two skills, and here is the first: STEEPNESS is acceleration. The rising ramp climbs 15 in 6 — steepness 2,5, the pull-away. The plateau lies level — steepness zero, the cruise. The falling ramp sheds 15 across 10 — steepness minus 1,5, the braking. Steeper means harder acceleration; the sign says climbing or falling. You can read the driver's feet straight off the shape: accelerator, cruising, brake.

And catch the classic misreading before it catches you: this is a portrait of the SPEEDOMETER, not a map of the route. The falling ramp does not mean the bus rolled backwards — it means the needle fell while the bus still rolled forward, slowing. Height is speed; the bus only reverses if the graph dives below the axis.

The questions for this section are up now. Match each piece of silhouette to the driver's feet — ramp, plateau, ramp — and hold the golden correction close: height means how fast, never how far.

## Subtopic: The Area That Pays Out in Metres

The second reading skill is the one that feels like a conjuring trick: the AREA under the velocity-time graph is the distance the bus covered.

Feel why before you calculate. Cruise at 15 metres per second for 30 seconds and you cover 15 times 30 — 450 metres. Now look at the graph: that multiplication is exactly the plateau's rectangle — height 15, width 30, area 450. Velocity times time IS area; the picture has been doing the multiplication silently all along.

The ramps obey the same logic with triangle arithmetic. Pull-away: a triangle 6 wide and 15 tall — half of 6 times 15 — 45 metres. The half makes physical sense too: accelerating from rest, the bus averaged only half its top speed, so it earned half of what a full-speed rectangle would pay. Braking: a triangle 10 wide and 15 tall — half of 10 times 15 — 75 metres of stopping distance.

Add the pieces: 45 plus 450 plus 75 — 570 metres for the entire trip, lifted off one drawing with primary-school geometry. No equations of motion, no memorised formulas: a triangle, a rectangle, a triangle.

That is the standing offer of the velocity-time graph, and it is the best bargain in Grade 10 physics: draw the silhouette once and it pays twice — steepness hands over every acceleration, area hands over every distance. When a wordy, confusing motion problem lands on your desk, the strongest opening move never changes: sketch the velocity-time graph and let the shape do the thinking.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now, and you are ready. Work each one on the drawing itself: label the heights and widths, read steepness for acceleration, slice the area into triangles and rectangles for distance — and let the picture carry the arithmetic.
