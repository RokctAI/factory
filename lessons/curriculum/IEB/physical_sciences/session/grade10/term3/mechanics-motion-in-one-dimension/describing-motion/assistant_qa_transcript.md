**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First up: if the walker genuinely walked 80 metres, how can physics say the displacement is only 40? It feels like the effort is being erased.

**Tutor:** Nothing is erased — two different questions are being answered, and each keeps its own books. Distance is the odometer question: how much ground was covered? Every metre of the 60 north and every metre of the 20 south counts, so 80 metres, and that number honours the effort completely. Displacement is the pigeon question: where did you end up, relative to where you began? Only the endpoints matter. The walker finished 40 metres north of the start, so the displacement is 40 metres north — and the 20 southward metres are not deleted, they are counted NEGATIVELY, because they moved the walker back towards the start. Plus 60 and minus 20 give plus 40. Both books balance; they simply record different things. The exam skill is noticing which book the question is asking you to open. "How far did she walk?" — odometer. "What is her displacement?" — pigeon. Same walk, both answers correct, marks only for the one that was asked.

***

**Thandi:** Next: someone divided 40 by 80 and got 0,5, but wrote just "0,5 metres per second" and lost a mark. The number was right — why the penalty?

**Tutor:** Because the question asked for a velocity, and a velocity is a vector — it is not fully stated until the direction is attached. Think about what "0,5 metres per second" alone leaves open: north? south? The number cannot say. The complete answer is 0,5 metres per second NORTH, and that final word is not decoration — it is half the physics. Here is a tidy way to hold the rule: scalars finish at the unit; vectors finish at the direction. Average speed, 1,0 metre per second — done, nothing missing. Average velocity — not done until the direction lands. The same discipline runs through everything ahead: forces, accelerations, momentum, all vectors, all requiring directions in final answers. Train the habit now on gentle problems like this walk, and it becomes automatic before the problems grow teeth. A good self-check at the end of any paper: scan every vector answer and ask, "does it end with a direction word?" Five seconds, and it rescues real marks.

***

**Thandi:** Then this one: the walker stopped for a juice break in the middle, someone says. Would we subtract that time before dividing?

**Tutor:** No — and this trap catches even strong learners. Average speed and average velocity are defined over the TOTAL time of the journey, and the clock is merciless: it runs while you rest. Suppose our walker had paused for 20 seconds mid-route, stretching the trip from 80 seconds to 100. The distance is still 80 metres and the displacement still 40 metres north, but the averages drop: average speed becomes 80 over 100, which is 0,8 metres per second, and average velocity becomes 40 over 100, which is 0,4 metres per second north. That is not an injustice — it is exactly what "average" means. An average smears the whole journey, moving and resting alike, into one steady rate that would produce the same result in the same time. If a question wants your moving pace only, it must ask for it explicitly. Read for the word "total", and when you draw a timeline for a problem, mark the rests on it — they are part of the journey's time even though they add nothing to its metres.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: is the bus-seat thing actually physics or just philosophy? Relative to the seat I'm still, relative to the road I'm moving — does any exam really care?

**Tutor:** It is bedrock physics, and yes, assessment cares — every signed number you write depends on it. There is no absolute standing-still anywhere in the universe: the bus moves over the road, the Earth spins, the whole planet orbits the Sun. So physics does not ask "is it really moving?" — a meaningless question — but "moving relative to WHICH declared frame?" Once you pick the frame, all the numbers become definite. Relative to the seat, your velocity is zero. Relative to the tar, it might be 15 metres per second forward. Both exact, both correct, different frames. Where this bites in your work is the sign convention: choosing north as positive IS choosing how your frame labels directions, and every plus and minus afterwards inherits that choice. That is why a first line reading "let north be positive" earns its place in every solution — it announces your frame like a chess player announcing a move. And it is why swapping frames mid-solution is fatal: the numbers from two different frames simply do not belong in the same equation.
