**Thandi:** Class questions before we finish! The first one is about the minus sign: is a negative acceleration always braking? Someone says their older sibling told them that's not true, and now the class is arguing.

**Tutor:** The sibling is right, and the argument is worth settling forever. The sign of an acceleration belongs to the FRAME, not to the idea of slowing down. In our bus problem we took the direction of motion as positive, so the braking phase came out negative — velocity positive, acceleration negative, opposing signs, so the speed shrinks. That is the slowing-down case. But now drop a ball and call upward positive. While the ball falls, its velocity is negative AND its acceleration is negative — the same sign — and the ball moves faster every second. A negative acceleration is speeding that object up. The reliable rule reads the two signs together: when acceleration and velocity share a sign, the object speeds up; when they carry opposite signs, it slows down. "Deceleration" is everyday language, not a physics quantity, which is why your working should say "the acceleration is minus 1,5 metres per second squared" and let the declared frame give the minus its meaning. Signs are directions in disguise — read them against the frame, never against the vibe of the word.

***

**Thandi:** Next: two people got 570 metres for the total displacement, but one of them divided by the total time and announced an average velocity of about 12,4 metres per second. The bus never went faster than 15 — is that answer even sensible?

**Tutor:** It is sensible, and checking it the way you just did — against the top speed — is exactly the habit I want. The trip took 6 plus 30 plus 10, which is 46 seconds, and 570 divided by 46 is about 12,4 metres per second. Notice what that number is NOT: it is not any speed the bus actually held at some moment. It is the single steady speed that would have covered the same 570 metres in the same 46 seconds. It must land below 15, because the bus spent its ramp-up and braking phases below top speed — an average is dragged down by every slow second. If your average had come out ABOVE the maximum on the graph, that would be an impossible answer and a sign to recheck the areas or the time. This is the quiet power of the velocity-time graph: it hands you the ingredients — total area, total width — and then referees your answer, because the average velocity must always sit between the graph's lowest and highest points.

***

**Thandi:** Then a graph one: on the position-time graph, why is the pull-away drawn as a curve, but on the velocity-time graph the very same pull-away is a straight line? Same motion, different shapes — it feels inconsistent.

**Tutor:** It feels inconsistent until you remember what each vertical axis is reporting, and then it becomes inevitable. During the pull-away the velocity grows by the same 2,5 metres per second every second — a steady, even change. On velocity-time axes, "steady, even change" draws as a straight rising line, because the quantity on the vertical axis is the velocity itself. But position behaves differently: with the bus moving faster each second, it covers more ground in each successive second — 1,25 metres in the first, then 3,75, then 6,25. Stack up ever-bigger gains and the position-time graph must bend upward, steepening — a curve. Same motion, two honest portraits of two different quantities. Here is the translation rule that keeps you safe: the gradient of the position-time graph at any moment equals the height of the velocity-time graph at that same moment. Steepening curve, rising line — perfectly consistent. The graphs disagree in shape precisely because they agree in physics.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: in an exam, is it worth the time to draw the graph if the question doesn't ask for one? Some people say sketching wastes minutes.

**Tutor:** Spend the minute — it buys back five. A velocity-time sketch is not decoration; it is a machine for organising the whole problem. The moment the silhouette is on paper, every phase is visible, every acceleration is a slope you can point at, and every displacement is a shape whose area you can compute with primary-school geometry — half base times height, length times width. No formula hunting, no wondering which equation fits which phase. It also catches errors before they cost marks: a phase you forgot shows up as a gap; a time you misread shows up as a wrong width; an impossible average shows up above the plateau. And when a question has multiple parts — accelerations here, distances there — one drawing answers all of them, so the minute is amortised across the entire question. My standing advice for any wordy motion problem: read it once, then draw time along the bottom, velocity up the side, and translate each sentence into a piece of silhouette. When the picture is complete, the physics is mostly done — what remains is measuring your own drawing.
