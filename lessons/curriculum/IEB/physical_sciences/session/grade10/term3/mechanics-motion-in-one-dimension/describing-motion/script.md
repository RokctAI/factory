# Part 1 — Expert

This session assembles the vocabulary of motion — frame of reference, position, distance, displacement, speed and velocity — the six ideas every later mechanics question silently assumes you own. One problem powers the whole hour: a learner walks 60 metres north along a straight pavement, then turns and walks 20 metres south, the whole trip taking 80 seconds. From that single walk we must extract the distance travelled, the displacement, the average speed and the average velocity — four answers, and two of them ambush almost everyone on first meeting.

## Subtopic: Frame of Reference and Position

Before motion can be discussed, physics must settle a quieter matter: where is the object right now? Position is location relative to a chosen reference point — an agreed zero. Strip away the reference point and position dissolves: sitting in a moving bus, you are motionless relative to your seat and racing along relative to the tar. Both statements are true; they simply live in different frames of reference.

A frame of reference is a reference point plus a chosen positive direction, working as a team. For our walk: let the starting spot be zero and let north be positive. Instantly every point on the pavement owns a number. Fifteen metres north of the start: plus 15 metres. Eight metres south of it: minus 8 metres. The sign carries the direction; the number carries the how-far.

Track our walker. Start: zero. After the northward leg: plus 60 metres. Then 20 metres south, and the position slips back to plus 40 metres. Final position: 40 metres north of the start. Position, notice, is a vector — magnitude and direction, folded into one signed number the moment the frame is declared.

Two disciplines belong in the first line of every solution. Declare the frame — the sentence "let north be positive" takes five seconds to write and rules every sign that follows. And never trade frames midstream: north positive in line one and south positive in line three breeds contradictions no marker can rescue.

The questions for this section are with you now. They probe the reference point, the sign-as-direction idea, the bus seat versus the tar, and the walker's final position of plus 40 metres.

## Subtopic: Distance versus Displacement

Now the pair that cuts every motion problem in two. Distance: the full length of the actual path — ground covered, direction ignored. A scalar. Displacement: the straight-line change in position between start and finish, direction included. A vector. One walk, two entirely different questions.

Compute both. Distance: 60 metres out plus 20 metres back — every metre is counted — 80 metres. Displacement: final position minus initial position — plus 40 minus zero — 40 metres north. Hold the two answers side by side: eighty metres of walking produced a net shift of only forty.

Symbols: distance is D; displacement is delta x, where delta always reads "change in", so delta x is the change in position. Both are measured in metres — the difference is not the unit but the cargo: displacement must ship with a direction, and a bare "40 metres" answer to a displacement question is incomplete and forfeits the mark.

Stretch the contrast to its limit, because assessments certainly will. One complete lap of a 300-metre track: distance 300 metres; displacement zero — the finish sits exactly on the start, so the change in position is nothing at all. Genuine effort, zero displacement. The two quantities only agree when the motion never turns around: walk 60 metres north and stop, and the distance and the displacement magnitude are both 60.

Take this section's questions now. They test the definitions as questions — ground covered versus change of position — the 80 and the 40 north, the lap that scores zero, and the single situation where the pair agree.

## Subtopic: Average Speed versus Average Velocity

Divide each of those answers by the time, and the split repeats one level up — as speed and velocity.

Average speed: distance divided by total time. A scalar, inheriting from distance. Substitute: 80 metres over 80 seconds — 1,0 metre per second. That is the ground-eating rate: metres of path per second of journey, no direction attached.

Average velocity: displacement divided by total time — delta x over delta t. A vector, inheriting the displacement's direction. Substitute: 40 metres north over 80 seconds — 0,5 metres per second, north. Half the speed, because a quarter of the walking was spent undoing earlier walking.

Both numbers are honest answers to different questions. How briskly was ground covered? One comma nought. How quickly did the position change, net? Nought comma five, north. When a question says "calculate the average velocity", it has chosen the second question on purpose — answering 1,0, the speed, earns nothing even with flawless arithmetic. Read the question's noun before reaching for the calculator.

Pin down one more consequence: average velocity can be zero while average speed is healthy. The full 300-metre lap in 75 seconds — average speed 300 over 75, which is 4 metres per second; average velocity zero over 75 — zero. Any journey that returns to its start gets velocity's verdict: no net motion. Speed records the effort; velocity records the outcome.

This section's questions are in front of you. They pair each formula with its parent — speed from distance, velocity from displacement — and work the 1,0, the 0,5 north, and the lap where four metres per second of speed carries zero velocity.

## Subtopic: The Full Method, the Classic Traps, and Where This Leads

The method for every describing-motion problem, in four steps. Step one: declare the frame — reference point and positive direction, in writing. Step two: follow the positions as signed numbers, leg by leg. Step three: compute distance along the path, but displacement from endpoints only — final minus initial. Step four: divide by the TOTAL time — speed from distance, velocity from displacement — and clip a direction onto every vector answer.

The traps, ranked by the marks they claim. Trap one: serving speed when velocity was ordered, or the reverse — the scalar-vector mix-up is the most assessed distinction in the topic. Trap two: building displacement by adding path lengths — displacement ignores the route and consults only the endpoints; 60 plus 20 is the distance, 60 minus 20 the displacement magnitude. Trap three: a vector answer with no direction — 0,5 metres per second is incomplete; 0,5 metres per second north is the answer. Trap four: dividing by the wrong time — averages use the total journey time, rest stops included; the clock keeps running while you catch your breath. Trap five: sign chaos from a frame that was never declared, or was quietly swapped.

Where does this vocabulary lead? Everywhere mechanics goes. Acceleration, next in this term, measures how velocity changes — hopeless if velocity itself is blurry. The equations of motion are written entirely in displacement and velocity. Grade 11 momentum is mass times velocity — the vector, never the speed. And the vertical projectile work waiting in Grade 12 lives or dies on signs declared exactly as you practised today. This is the alphabet; everything afterwards is spelling.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the four steps, the five traps — especially the scalar-vector swaps — and the reason total time includes the rest stop. Answer them as method, not from memory.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same walk again — but now with a bus stop, an odometer and a pigeon, the whole topic in three everyday pictures.

## Subtopic: Where Are You, Relative to What

Explain to a friend where you live and listen to your own words: "from the bus stop, three houses towards the river." Nobody hands out a location from nowhere — there is always a from, an agreed marker. Physics calls the marker a reference point, and position is nothing more than where you are, measured from it.

Add one more agreement — which way counts as forward — and the frame of reference is complete. Make the bus stop zero and towards-the-river positive, and the whole street turns into numbers: the spaza shop three houses along is plus three; the soccer field two houses the other way is minus two. The minus is not an insult; it is simply the other direction, written compactly.

Our problem does exactly this. The walker's starting spot is zero; north is positive. Walk 60 metres north: you stand at plus 60. Turn and walk 20 metres south: you slide back to plus 40. That final signed number is a complete address — forty metres from the start, on the north side — one number carrying both how far and which way.

And the moving-bus riddle dissolves. Relative to your seat you are still; relative to the tar you are moving. Neither claim is wrong — they use different reference points, and each is a legitimate frame. Physics never asks which frame is "the real one"; it asks you to announce the one you are using, then stick with it to the end.

Your questions for this section are ready now. Keep the bus stop in your head: every position needs its from, every sign is a direction, and the one-line declaration at the top keeps all the arithmetic below it honest.

## Subtopic: The Odometer and the Pigeon

Two instruments can measure one journey, and you know both already.

The odometer in a car counts every metre the wheels turn — to the shop, around the corner, home again — and its total only ever climbs. That is distance: ground covered, whole route, no interest in direction. Eighty metres of walking is eighty on the odometer, no arguments.

The pigeon ignores your route entirely. Released where you finished, it flies straight back to where you began — and that flight's length and direction measure how far you actually ended up from your start. That is displacement: start to finish in a straight line, direction attached. Our walker's pigeon flies a mere 40 metres, aiming south back at the start — which tells us the displacement was 40 metres north.

One walk, two truthful readings: odometer 80, pigeon 40 north. The gap between them is the turning back — every metre of the southward leg fattened the odometer while trimming the pigeon's flight. And the famous extreme: run one full lap of the track and the odometer boasts 300 metres while the pigeon never leaves its perch — start and finish are the same spot, displacement zero. The effort was real; the net change is nothing. The only journey where the two instruments agree is one that never turns around.

The questions for this section are up now. Put each situation through the two instruments: what does the odometer read, and what does the pigeon fly? Master those two readings and distance versus displacement never troubles you again.

## Subtopic: Two Speedometers for One Walk

Divide each instrument's reading by the journey time, and you own the two averages that questions love to swap.

Odometer over time: 80 metres of ground in 80 seconds — 80 over 80 is 1,0 metre per second. That is average speed: how hungrily the ground was eaten, direction ignored. It is the number your tired legs would agree with.

Pigeon over time: a net change of 40 metres north across the same 80 seconds — 40 over 80 is 0,5 metres per second, north. That is average velocity: the net rate of change of position, direction included. It is the number that says how far you actually got.

One walk, two speedometers, and the answers differ by a factor of two — both correct. The gap tells a story: a quarter of the walking was spent cancelling earlier walking. A delivery scooter weaving through town all afternoon and ending near its depot can post a big average speed beside a velocity of nearly nothing; the lap runner logs four metres per second of speed with a velocity of exactly zero, because the pigeon stays home.

So read the question's noun like a price tag. Speed wanted? Odometer over time. Velocity wanted? Pigeon over time — and speak the direction every time, because a velocity without direction is half an answer. One final habit completes the kit: divide by the whole journey time, juice breaks included; the clock never pauses just because you did.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now, and you are ready. Two instruments, two divisions, one declared direction — file each question under odometer or pigeon before touching the calculator, and this topic turns into the friendliest marks you will ever collect.
