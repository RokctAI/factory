## Subtopic: Introduction to River Profiles

I am not here to tell you stories; I am here to give you the precise geographical language and tools you need to interpret landscapes exactly as examiners do. We are looking at Geomorphology today, specifically the anatomy of a river.

Our focus is river profiles. A river profile is a two-dimensional cross-section of a river. We examine two distinct types of profiles: longitudinal and transverse. The longitudinal profile shows the river from its source to its mouth, mapping the gradient and elevation changes over distance. The transverse, or cross-profile, slices across the river channel and valley to show its width and depth.

You must master the terminology. 'Base level' is the lowest level to which a river can erode its bed. The permanent base level is always sea level. However, a river often encounters temporary base levels—such as a lake, a resistant layer of rock, or a dam—which halt downward erosion locally.

When you study a longitudinal profile, you are looking for evidence of the balance between erosion and deposition. Does the river flow smoothly, or is its path interrupted by sudden drops like waterfalls? This distinction forms the basis of our next crucial concept: the difference between graded and ungraded profiles. We will explore exactly what defines these two states and how to recognize them.

## Subtopic: Graded vs Ungraded River Profiles

A river's ultimate goal is to achieve a state of dynamic equilibrium, where the rate of erosion perfectly matches the rate of deposition. When a river achieves this balance, its longitudinal profile becomes a smooth, concave-upwards curve from source to mouth. We call this a graded profile. In a graded river, the velocity is just sufficient to transport the sediment load without actively eroding the bed or depositing material.

However, nature is rarely in perfect equilibrium. Most rivers possess an ungraded profile. An ungraded profile is irregular. It displays knickpoints—sharp changes in gradient caused by resistant rock outcrops, waterfalls, or rapids. An ungraded river is out of balance. It possesses excess energy and must actively erode these irregularities, striving to smooth out its channel and reach that graded state.

Let us differentiate them clearly for your answer sheet. A graded profile is smooth and concave, representing a balance between erosion and deposition. An ungraded profile is uneven and interrupted by knickpoints, representing a river that is still actively eroding to reach equilibrium.

If you encounter a profile with multiple knickpoints on a topography map, you are looking at an ungraded river. Your task is to identify the temporary base levels causing these interruptions. Often, these knickpoints hold the key to understanding the river's history, leading us to our next major process: river rejuvenation.

## Subtopic: The Process of River Rejuvenation

A river, once graded, does not necessarily remain so permanently. If the land surface undergoes a significant tectonic uplift, or if there is a global drop in sea level—known as a eustatic change—the river's permanent base level drops relative to the land.

When the base level drops, the river suddenly gains renewed potential energy. Its gradient becomes steeper, and its velocity increases. This renewed energy forces the river to abandon its previous balance and begin active downward erosion once again. This renewed period of vigorous vertical erosion is called river rejuvenation.

Rejuvenation leaves distinct, measurable marks on the landscape. A rejuvenated river cuts deeply into its own flood plain. Because the downward erosion happens faster than lateral erosion, the river forms deep, steep-sided valleys known as incised meanders, or entrenched meanders.

Furthermore, as the river cuts down, the remnants of its old, higher flood plain are left behind as stepped surfaces on the valley sides. We term these features river terraces. When you see a pair of river terraces on a cross-section, they are the undeniable signature of river rejuvenation. The river has 'woken up' and carved a new path.

## Subtopic: Constructing the Rejuvenation Diagram

We will now construct the standard diagram for river rejuvenation. You must be able to reproduce this accurately.

First, I draw the initial, pre-rejuvenation profile. Notice the broad, gentle flood plain. This is a river near the end of its course, meandering sluggishly.

Now, imagine a tectonic uplift event. The entire landmass rises. I will draw a dashed line below the original profile to indicate the new, lower base level.

The river responds immediately. Its energy spikes, and it begins rapid downward erosion. Look at the diagram as I draw the new channel. The river cuts deeply into the original flood plain. It does not widen the valley; it cuts straight down, creating a steep, V-shaped gorge within the older, broader valley.

Observe the features we have created. The flat areas elevated above the new, active channel are the river terraces. Label them clearly. These were once the flood plain. The steep sides of the newly eroded channel form the incised meander. The point where the old profile meets the newly eroding, steeper profile is the knickpoint, which will slowly migrate upstream over time.

When asked to explain rejuvenation with a labelled diagram, this is the sequence you must present. Show the initial broad valley, the rapid vertical incision, and correctly label the resulting terraces, incised meanders, and the knickpoint. This is the precise, geographical proof of rejuvenation.
