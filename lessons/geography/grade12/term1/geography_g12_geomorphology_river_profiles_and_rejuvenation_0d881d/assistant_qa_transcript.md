# Mandy Q&A Transcript

**Student:** I keep getting confused between longitudinal and transverse profiles. How do I remember which is which?

**Mandy:** Oh, this is a classic mix-up! Think of "longitudinal" like the word "long." It's looking at the river along its whole length, from the very top of the mountain all the way down to the ocean. "Transverse" is like slicing a loaf of bread across the middle—it just shows you what a slice of the valley looks like from bank to bank at one specific spot.

**Student:** Dr Molefe mentioned "eustatic change." What does that actually mean? Do we have to use that exact word?

**Mandy:** Yeah, "eustatic change" sounds super technical, but it literally just means a global change in sea level. Like if ice caps melt and the ocean rises, or if there's an ice age and the ocean drops. It's good to use the exact word in the exam because examiners give marks for geographical terminology. Just remember: Eustatic = Sea level change. Tectonic = Land lifting or dropping.

**Student:** Why do river terraces always come in pairs in the diagrams?

**Mandy:** That's a great question! When the river cuts down into its old flood plain, it leaves the edges of that old, flat plain high up on both sides of the new valley it's carving. Since it cuts down roughly in the middle, you're left with a "step" on the left bank and a "step" on the right bank. So, one old flood plain becomes two terraces!

**Student:** If a river has a waterfall, is it graded or ungraded?

**Mandy:** Definitely ungraded! Remember, a graded river is perfectly smooth and has balanced out all its lumps and bumps. A waterfall is a giant "bump" in the river's bed (what Dr Molefe calls a knickpoint). The river will keep eroding that waterfall over thousands of years until it eventually smoothes it out into a graded profile.
