# Q&A with Mandy: Mid-latitude Cyclones

**Student:** I always get confused about the rotation. Does it spin clockwise or anti-clockwise?

**Mandy:** I used to mess this up in tests all the time! Here is the trick: for South Africa (which is in the Southern Hemisphere), mid-latitude cyclones *always* spin clockwise. Just imagine you are turning a steering wheel to the right. If they ask about the Northern Hemisphere (like Europe), it’s the opposite. But for our exams, stick to clockwise.

**Student:** Why do we call it a 'cold front' if there is warm air involved too?

**Mandy:** Good question. The whole system has both warm and cold air, but the 'front' is just the leading edge of the air mass that is doing the moving. When the cold air is aggressively pushing forward into the warm air, we call that line the cold front. When the warm air is trying to push forward over the cold air, we call that line the warm front.

**Student:** Why does the cold front cause such heavy rain, but the warm front doesn't?

**Mandy:** Think about a bulldozer versus a gentle hill. The cold air is super heavy. It bulldozes underneath the warm air and shoves it straight up into the sky really fast. Fast rising air = massive clouds and heavy rain. The warm air is light, so it can't bulldoze. It just slowly slides up over the cold air like walking up a gentle hill. Slow rising air = thin clouds and gentle rain.

**Student:** What does 'occlusion' actually mean? It's a weird word.

**Mandy:** It just means 'blocked' or 'closed off'. In the occluded stage, the cold front at the back is moving so fast that it catches up to the warm front. When they smash together, all the warm air is squeezed out and lifted completely off the ground. The warm air is 'closed off' from the surface. Without that warm air touching the ground, the storm loses its energy and dies.
