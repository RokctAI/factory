## Subtopic: Introduction and Definition of the Multiplier

Welcome, grade 12s. I am Ms Mahlangu, and this is Economics. Today, we are mastering a concept that drives macroeconomic policy: the multiplier.

In Economics, precision is everything. We do not guess; we calculate. We do not assume; we prove. We will begin, as always, with the exact CAPS definition. The MULTIPLIER EFFECT is the phenomenon where an initial change in spending, or an injection, leads to a proportionately larger final change in the national income.

State, explain, illustrate. We have stated the definition. Now, let us explain the mechanism.

You already know the circular flow model. You know that money flows between households, firms, the government, and the foreign sector. You also know about INJECTIONS, which are introductions of income into the circular flow, such as government spending, investment, or exports. And you know about LEAKAGES, which are withdrawals of income from the circular flow, such as savings, taxes, and imports.

When an injection occurs—say, the South African government spends on new infrastructure—that money goes to construction firms. Those firms pay their workers. Those workers go to the shops and spend a portion of their wages. The shop owners then have more income, which they also spend. One initial injection creates a chain reaction of spending, generating multiple rounds of income. That is why the final change in national income is larger than the initial injection. Cause, and effect.

## Subtopic: The Multiplier Formula and Marginal Propensities

To calculate exactly how much the national income will change, we need to understand consumer behaviour. Specifically, we need to know what proportion of additional income households will spend, and what proportion they will save.

This introduces our next critical definition: the MARGINAL PROPENSITY TO CONSUME, or MPC. The MPC is the proportion of any additional income that is spent on the consumption of goods and services. If you receive an extra Rand, the MPC tells us exactly how many cents of that Rand you will spend.

Conversely, we have the MARGINAL PROPENSITY TO SAVE, or MPS. This is the proportion of any additional income that is saved. Because you can only either spend or save your disposable income, the MPC and the MPS must always add up to exactly 1.

The formula for the multiplier, which we represent with the letter 'k', is based on these propensities. The formula is: k equals 1 divided by open bracket 1 minus MPC close bracket.
k = 1 / (1 - MPC).

Since 1 minus the Marginal Propensity to Consume is exactly equal to the Marginal Propensity to Save, we can also write the formula as: k equals 1 divided by MPS. Or, if we include taxes and imports as leakages, we use the Marginal Propensity to Withdraw, giving us k = 1 / MPW.

For your exams, you must write the formula exactly as it is required by the problem.

## Subtopic: Working the Example Problem - Calculating the Multiplier

Let us put this theory into practice. Examine the problem before us: "If the marginal propensity to consume is 0,75, calculate the multiplier and the total change in national income resulting from a R100 million injection."

This is a classic exam question. We will solve it systematically. Step one: state your given variables. We are given the MPC, which is 0,75. We are also given the initial injection, which is R100 million.

Step two: write down the correct formula. We need to calculate the multiplier first.
k = 1 / (1 - MPC).

Step three: substitute the given value into the formula.
k = 1 / (1 - 0,75).

Step four: calculate the denominator. 1 minus 0,75 gives us 0,25. This means the Marginal Propensity to Save is 0,25. For every extra Rand earned, 25 cents is saved, and 75 cents is spent.

Step five: final calculation.
k = 1 / 0,25.
Therefore, the multiplier, k, is equal to 4.

What does this number mean? It means that for every 1 Rand injected into the economy, the total national income will increase by 4 Rand. The initial injection is multiplied four times through the successive rounds of spending.

## Subtopic: Total Change in National Income and Summary

Now for the final part of our exam question. We must calculate the total change in national income resulting from the R100 million injection.

The formula for the change in national income is simple:
Change in National Income (which we write as delta Y) equals the Multiplier (k) multiplied by the initial change in injections (delta J).

Let us substitute our values.
Change in National Income = 4 multiplied by R100 million.
The calculation is straightforward: 4 times 100 million gives us 400 million.

But remember, this is Economics. A number without a unit is meaningless. Your final answer must explicitly state the currency.
The total change in national income is R400 million.

Let us review what we have achieved. We stated the definition of the multiplier effect. We identified the mechanism—how an initial injection creates successive rounds of spending depending on the Marginal Propensity to Consume. We used the correct formula, k = 1 / (1 - MPC), substituted our values perfectly, and arrived at a multiplier of 4. Finally, we proved that an initial government injection of R100 million expands to increase the national income by R400 million.
