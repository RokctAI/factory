## Subtopic: 1. Review of Demand and Supply Curves

Today we examine the DYNAMICS OF MARKETS, specifically focusing on DEMAND AND SUPPLY EQUILIBRIUM. In Economics, a MARKET is any place or mechanism where buyers and sellers interact to trade goods and services.

Before we identify equilibrium, we must lock in our understanding of the two foundational forces: Demand and Supply. Let us define our terms.

DEMAND is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at various prices over a given period. The LAW OF DEMAND states that, ceteris paribus—all other factors held constant—as the price of a good increases, the quantity demanded decreases. This inverse relationship gives us our downward-sloping Demand curve, which we label 'D'.

SUPPLY, on the other hand, is the quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to offer for sale at various prices over a given period. The LAW OF SUPPLY states that, ceteris paribus, as the price of a good increases, the quantity supplied also increases. This positive relationship gives us our upward-sloping Supply curve, labeled 'S'.

In the South African economy, whether we are analyzing the price of a loaf of bread in a supermarket or the cost of data on mobile networks, these two curves govern the behavior of consumers and producers respectively.

## Subtopic: 2. Market Equilibrium

When we place both the Demand curve and the Supply curve on the same set of axes, we find a point of intersection. This is MARKET EQUILIBRIUM.

EQUILIBRIUM occurs where the quantity demanded by consumers exactly equals the quantity supplied by producers. The price at this intersection is known as the EQUILIBRIUM PRICE, and the quantity traded is the EQUILIBRIUM QUANTITY.

At this exact price, the market clears. There is no shortage—meaning quantity demanded does not exceed quantity supplied—and there is no surplus—meaning quantity supplied does not exceed quantity demanded.

If the current market price were set above the equilibrium price, producers would supply more than consumers wish to buy, creating a SURPLUS. Market forces would naturally push the price downward until equilibrium is restored. Conversely, if the price were below equilibrium, a SHORTAGE would occur, driving prices upward as consumers compete for limited goods.

Thus, equilibrium is a state of rest in the market, achieved naturally through the price mechanism.

## Subtopic: 3. Example Problem: Shift in Demand

Let us apply this theory to a practical example. The problem at hand is to show on one graph how an increase in consumer income shifts the demand curve for beef, and to identify the new equilibrium price and quantity.

First, we establish our initial state. We draw our axes: the vertical axis represents the Price of Beef in Rands, and the horizontal axis represents the Quantity of Beef in kilograms.

We draw the initial downward-sloping Demand curve, labeled D1, and the initial upward-sloping Supply curve, labeled S1.

Their intersection gives us our initial equilibrium point, E1. From E1, we trace a line to the price axis to find our initial equilibrium price, P1, and to the quantity axis to find our initial equilibrium quantity, Q1.

Now, consider the shock to the market: an increase in consumer income. Beef is generally considered a NORMAL GOOD. A NORMAL GOOD is a good for which demand increases when consumer income increases. As South African households find themselves with more disposable income, perhaps due to tax cuts or economic growth, their ability to purchase beef increases at every given price point.

Because this change is caused by a factor other than the price of beef itself, it results in a SHIFT of the demand curve, rather than a movement along it. Specifically, the demand curve shifts to the right, from D1 to D2.

Notice that the supply curve, S1, remains unchanged, because the cost of producing beef has not been altered by the consumers' income change.

Let us examine the new state of the market. Our new demand curve, D2, intersects our original supply curve, S1, at a new, higher point. This is our new equilibrium, E2.

If we trace from E2 to our axes, we find the new equilibrium price, P2, which is higher than P1. We also find the new equilibrium quantity, Q2, which is greater than Q1.

The conclusion is clear: an increase in consumer income has led to a higher equilibrium price and a greater equilibrium quantity of beef traded in the market.

## Subtopic: 4. Summary and Sign-off

To summarize our analysis:

First, the interaction of Demand and Supply determines the market equilibrium, where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.

Second, changes in non-price factors, such as consumer income, cause the entire demand curve to shift.

Finally, as demonstrated by the South African beef market, an outward shift in demand—while supply remains constant—results in both a higher equilibrium price and a higher equilibrium quantity.

Mastering these graph mechanics is non-negotiable for economic analysis. You must be precise with your labels and clear on the distinction between a movement along a curve and a shift of a curve.

Study the definitions, practice drawing the graphs, and the logic of the market will become second nature. Until our next session, keep your minds sharp.
