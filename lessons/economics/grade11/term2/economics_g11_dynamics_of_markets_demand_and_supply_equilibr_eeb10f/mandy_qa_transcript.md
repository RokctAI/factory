**Student:** Mandy, I’m still a bit confused. Why didn't the supply curve shift too? If people have more money, won't suppliers just make more beef?

**Mandy:** That’s a really smart question! It’s easy to get that mixed up. So, it comes down to *why* we draw the curves. The supply curve tells us how much beef farmers are willing to sell *at different prices*.

When consumers got more income, they pushed the price up (that shift from D1 to D2). Because the price went up, farmers *did* produce more beef! But they did that by sliding *up* their existing supply curve.

We only SHIFT the supply curve if something changes for the farmers that has nothing to do with price—like if a new machine made raising cows much cheaper, or a drought made it much harder. Since their costs didn’t change, the line itself stays put. Make sense?

**Student:** Ah, okay! So income only shifts demand. What about the "ceteris paribus" thing the Grandmaster mentioned?

**Mandy:** Yes! "Ceteris paribus" is just fancy Latin for "holding all other things constant". In economics, the real world is messy. If we try to look at income changing, *and* a drought happening, *and* the price of chicken dropping all at exactly the same time, our graph would just be scribbles.

So, we use "ceteris paribus" to freeze everything else in place. We say, "If *only* income changes, and everything else stays exactly the same, what happens?" It lets us solve one piece of the puzzle at a time!

**Student:** Okay, that makes it less scary. One more thing: I missed a question on the quiz about a surplus. How does that happen?

**Mandy:** No stress, that’s super common. Imagine equilibrium is a price of R100 for beef. That’s where buyers and sellers are happy. But imagine the government stepped in and said, "No, beef must be sold for R150!"

At R150, farmers are thrilled! They bring tons of beef to the market. But consumers? They think R150 is too expensive, so they buy chicken instead.

Now, there is tons of beef sitting on the shelves, going bad, because nobody wants to buy it at that high price. That leftover beef is a **surplus**. It only happens when the price is forced *above* the happy equilibrium point.
