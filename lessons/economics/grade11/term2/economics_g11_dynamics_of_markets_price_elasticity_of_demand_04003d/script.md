## Subtopic: The Taxi Fare Reality Check

Ey, welcome. You see, petrol goes up, watch what happens to your taxi fare. That's economics, right there on the street.

Let’s talk about a word they love in the textbooks: elasticity. Before you start sweating over graphs and big terms, think about your morning commute. You get to the rank, and the marshal says the fare to town just went up from R15 to R20. What happens? Some people will grumble but still get in the taxi, because they have to be at work by 8 AM. But others? They turn around, go to the bus stop, or they decide to share a lift with their neighbor.

When the price changed, the number of people wanting to take the taxi changed. That reaction, my friend, is what we call elasticity. It's just a fancy word for "how much do people jump when the price bites them?" If people run away fast when the price goes up, we say the demand is elastic—it stretches a lot. If they just stand there and take the price increase because they have no other choice, we say the demand is inelastic. It's stiff. It doesn't move much.

Today, we are looking at PRICE ELASTICITY OF DEMAND. In our CAPS economics, that means we are measuring exactly how much the quantity demanded of a product reacts to a change in its price.

## Subtopic: Defining Price Elasticity of Demand

Let’s get the official definition down so you can score full marks when the examiner asks. PRICE ELASTICITY OF DEMAND is a measure of the responsiveness, or sensitivity, of the quantity demanded of a good or service to a change in its price.

Think of a rubber band. If you pull it and it stretches easily, it is highly elastic. If you pull it and it barely moves, it is inelastic.

Now, we already know the Law of Demand, right? When the price goes up, the quantity demanded goes down. When the price goes down, the quantity demanded goes up. That is just basic survival at the spaza shop. But Price Elasticity of Demand asks a deeper question: *By how much* does it go down?

If the local bakery raises the price of a loaf of bread by R2, will they lose five customers, or will they lose fifty customers? The answer to that question tells the bakery owner if raising the price was a smart business move or a terrible mistake. This is why economists and business owners care about elasticity. It tells you how sensitive your customers are to your prices.

## Subtopic: The Elasticity Formula

Now, how do we measure this? We can't just guess. We need a formula. And don't let the maths scare you; it’s just basic percentages. We are comparing two things: the percentage change in quantity demanded, and the percentage change in price.

The formula for Price Elasticity of Demand—let’s call it PED—is this:
PED equals the percentage change in quantity demanded, divided by the percentage change in price.

On the board, we write it as:
PED = % Δ Qd / % Δ P

That little triangle is called Delta, and it just means "change in".
To find the percentage change for anything, whether it's the quantity or the price, you use a simple rule from the streets. You take the change in the amount, divide it by the original amount, and multiply by 100.

So, % Δ Qd is the change in quantity divided by the original quantity, times 100.
And % Δ P is the change in price divided by the original price, times 100.

Remember this rule: Quantity is always on top. Why? Because the change in quantity is the *reaction*, and the change in price is the *cause*. We want to see how big the reaction is compared to the cause. If you put price on top, the whole calculation will be upside down, just like a taxi that took a corner too fast! Quantity on top, always.

## Subtopic: Solving the Calculation

Let's do some real work.

Here is the problem: "The price of a product rises from R10 to R12 and quantity demanded falls from 100 to 80 units. Calculate the price elasticity of demand and classify it."

Alright, step one. Don't rush. Let's find our original and new numbers.
Original Price, P1, is R10.
New Price, P2, is R12.
What is the change in price? It went from R10 to R12, so the change is R2.

Now for quantity.
Original Quantity, Q1, is 100 units.
New Quantity, Q2, is 80 units.
What is the change in quantity? It went from 100 down to 80. So the change is 20 units. In economics, we usually drop the negative sign when calculating price elasticity of demand because we already know the relationship is inverse. We just want the size of the reaction. So, the change is 20.

Step two. Let's calculate the percentage change for both.
Percentage change in Price:
That is the change in price (R2) divided by the original price (R10), multiplied by 100.
(2 / 10) * 100.
2 divided by 10 is 0,2. Times 100 gives us 20%. So the price went up by 20%.

Percentage change in Quantity Demanded:
That is the change in quantity (20) divided by the original quantity (100), multiplied by 100.
(20 / 100) * 100.
20 divided by 100 is 0,2. Times 100 gives us 20%. So the quantity demanded fell by 20%.

Step three. Put it into our PED formula.
PED = % Δ Qd / % Δ P
PED = 20% / 20%
20 divided by 20 is exactly 1.

So, our Price Elasticity of Demand is 1. We have calculated the number. Now, what does this number mean?

## Subtopic: Classifying Elasticity Categories

We found that our PED is 1. We need to classify it. In economics, we group the answers into different categories based on how big the number is.

If your answer is greater than 1, we call it PRICE ELASTIC. That means the percentage change in quantity is bigger than the percentage change in price. The customers are very sensitive. A small change in price causes a massive reaction. Think of luxury goods, or things with lots of substitutes, like different brands of cold drink. If one brand gets too expensive, you just buy another one. Big reaction.

If your answer is less than 1—so it's a zero-point-something—we call it PRICE INELASTIC. The percentage change in quantity is smaller than the percentage change in price. The customers are not very sensitive. Even if the price jumps, they still buy almost the same amount. Think of basic necessities, like bread, or petrol for your car. You complain, but you still pay. Small reaction.

But what about our example? Our answer was exactly 1. When the answer is exactly 1, we call it UNITARY ELASTICITY. This means the percentage change in quantity is exactly equal to the percentage change in price. In our problem, the price went up by 20%, and the quantity demanded fell by exactly 20%. It is a perfect one-to-one balance.

So to answer the final part of our example problem: the price elasticity of demand is 1, and we classify it as unitary elasticity.

And that is it, my friends. Whether you are selling tomatoes at the rank or managing a big factory, elasticity just tells you if your customers are going to run away when you raise prices, or if they are going to stay put. Understand that, and you understand business.
