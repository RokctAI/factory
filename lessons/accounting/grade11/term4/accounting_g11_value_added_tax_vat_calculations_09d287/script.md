# Accounting Grade 11: Value Added Tax - VAT Calculations

## Subtopic: The Spaza Shop Cash Box (Intro to VAT)

Today, we are going to talk about something that touches every single person in this country, whether they are a big corporate business owner sitting in a fancy office in the city, or just a gogo buying a cold drink from my spaza shop. We are talking about Value Added Tax. Most people just call it VAT.

Now, don't let the word "tax" scare you. I know when people hear about tax, their eyes glaze over, they get nervous, and they think it is far too complicated for them to understand. But I promise you, your local spaza shop already does double-entry bookkeeping, even if you don't know it yet. Let me show you exactly where, and how VAT fits into our everyday lives, step by step.

What is VAT, really? In simple terms, it just means the government, through the South African Revenue Service—we call them SARS—wants a little slice of the pie when we buy or sell things. They use this money to run the country, to build roads, schools, clinics, and hospitals. But SARS cannot stand at the till of every single shop in the country to collect their coins. They are too busy for that. So, what do they do? They make the business owners do the hard work for them! When you run a registered business, you become a tax collector for SARS.

Think about my spaza shop. When a customer walks in and buys a loaf of bread or a bag of sugar, they hand me money. That money goes straight into my cash box, my tin. It feels very good to hear those coins drop, doesn't it? But here is the big secret you must understand for Accounting: not all that money in the tin belongs to me. A little piece of it belongs to SARS—the tax man. I am just holding it for them temporarily.

The law says I have to add VAT on top of my normal selling price. The standard rate in South Africa is 15%. So, if I decide my business needs a certain amount of money to cover my costs and make a little profit, I must take that amount, add an extra 15% for SARS, and charge the customer the new, bigger total.

Let's learn two very important words that you will see in every exam paper. If we have an amount *before* we add the tax man's share, we call that the **VAT exclusive amount**. Exclusive means it excludes the VAT. It is just the business's money, the pure price of the goods. When we add the VAT on top, the new, bigger amount that the customer actually pays at the till is the **VAT inclusive amount**. Inclusive means the VAT is included inside there. It is the full final price.

Let's look at a real example. Imagine a big bag of maize meal is marked at R2 875 on the shelf. The label says this price *includes* VAT at 15%. So the R2 875 is our inclusive amount. The customer brings the maize meal to the counter, gives me R2 875 in cash, and I put it all into my tin. But how much of that is mine, and how much belongs to SARS? We cannot just guess. In accounting, we must be exact. Numbers never lie, but we must do the right calculation to find the truth.

## Subtopic: Extracting the VAT (The Calculation)

We need to calculate two different things from that R2 875 sitting in the tin: the VAT amount (the exact piece that goes to SARS), and the price excluding VAT (the pure sales amount I actually get to keep to run my business).

Now, pay very close attention to me, because a lot of learners make a mistake right here. A very common, very dangerous mistake. They look at the R2 875, they grab their calculators, and they simply take 15% of the R2 875. They multiply by 15 over 100. No, my child, you cannot do that! If you do that in the final exam, the marker will put a big red cross on your page and you will lose easy marks. Why? Because that inclusive amount is not 100%.

Think about the everyday logic of the spaza shop. My original price, the exclusive price, was my whole pie. That was 100%. That was the money I needed. Then, the law told me to add another 15% on top for SARS. So the total amount the customer pays, the inclusive amount, represents my 100% plus SARS's 15%. It represents exactly 115%.

So, when a customer gives you an inclusive amount like R2 875, that big number is 115%. You cannot find 15% of a 115% number by just multiplying by 15 over 100. It simply does not work mathematically!

To find the VAT amount correctly, we use a simple, beautiful rule: we take the amount we *want* (the 15% for SARS), and divide it by the percentage we *have* (the 115% total).

So, our golden formula is:
Amount given multiplied by (what we want / what we have).

Let's do our maize meal example properly. We have R2 875. We know this is an inclusive price, so it is our 115% amount. We want to find the exact VAT amount, which is 15%. So, we multiply the amount by 15 over 115.
R2 875 × (15 / 115).

Get your calculator out and do it with me right now. Don't just watch me do the work. 2875 times 15, divided by 115. What do you get? That gives us R375.
So, the VAT amount is R375. That is the exact money that goes into the tin, but strictly belongs to SARS.

Now, what is the price *excluding* VAT? This is the easy part. It is just common sense. It is the total money in the tin, minus the tax man's share. You just take it away.
R2 875 (the total money received) minus R375 (the VAT we just calculated).
That leaves us with R2 500.
So, the price excluding VAT is R2 500. That's my pure sales amount! That is the money the business actually earned from selling the maize meal.

Let's do one more quick, smaller example just to make sure this rule is stuck in your head forever. Let's say a customer buys a crate of cold drinks for a party. The inclusive price they pay at the till is R230. Remember, because it includes VAT, that R230 represents 115%. How much is the VAT in there? We use our rule. What we want over what we have. We want 15, we have 115.
So, R230 multiplied by (15 / 115). Put it in the calculator... it gives us exactly R30. The VAT on the cold drinks is R30. And my pure sales amount? R230 total minus R30 VAT gives me R200. Simple, common sense accounting!

## Subtopic: Formal Ledger Accounts (Double Entry)

Now, we know the amounts for our big bag of maize meal: R2 875 total cash received, R375 for SARS, and R2 500 for the business itself. But how does this look in our formal ledger accounts? Because when a business grows up, we cannot just keep money mixed up in a tin. We must record every single movement of money in our General Ledger using double-entry bookkeeping. For every single debit, there must be an equal credit.

Let's think carefully about what just happened in our spaza shop. We sold goods for cash, so the money came straight into our bank account. The source document for this transaction is our Cash Register Roll, because we rang the sale up on the till.

We received a total of R2 875 from the customer. So, we will **debit** the **Bank account** with R2 875. Why a debit? You must always ask yourself why before you write anything down. Because Bank is an asset account. Assets increase on the debit side. We have more money sitting in our bank account now, so we debit it.

But where did this money come from? We have debited R2 875, so we must find exactly R2 875 worth of credit entries to balance our books. This incoming money is split into two separate parts, as we calculated earlier.

First, let's look at the business's own money. Remember, we calculated that R2 500 is our actual, pure sales income, exclusive of VAT. So, we will **credit** the **Sales account** with R2 500. Why? Because Sales is an income account. Income increases on the credit side, which in turn increases the owner's equity. The business has earned R2 500 of true income.

Second, what about the remaining R375? That is the VAT. That is money we owe directly to SARS. When you owe somebody money in accounting, what is that called? It is a liability! We record this debt in a special liability account called VAT Control. We will **credit** the **VAT Control account** with R375. Why? Because we owe this money to SARS, so our liability is increasing, and we know that liabilities increase on the credit side.

Let's step back and check our double-entry logic, just like a good accountant always does at the end of the day:
We have a Debit to the Bank account: R2 875.
We have a Credit to the Sales account: R2 500.
We have a Credit to the VAT Control account: R375.

Let's add our credit entries together. R2 500 plus R375 equals R2 875. Does our total debit equal our total credits? Yes! The books balance perfectly. The spaza shop's financial records are clean, transparent, and correct.

Before we finish up for today, I want to make a very important point on business ethics. When you collect that R375, you might be tempted to look at it sitting in your bank account and think, "Times are tough, maybe I can just use this cash to buy more stock, or pay myself a little bonus." No, my child! Always ensure that the VAT you collect from your customers is strictly recorded in that VAT Control account and paid over to SARS exactly when it is due. Keeping that money for the business is illegal, and it is highly unethical. You are stealing from the country. A good, strong business is an honest business.

Keep practicing those calculations, don't let the percentages confuse you, and always remember the golden rule of 115! The math will protect you.
