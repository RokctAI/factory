## Subtopic: Welcome and Introduction

[Prof Mokoena looking directly into the camera, deliberate pace.]

Numbers never lie. I am Prof Mokoena. Welcome.
Today, our focus is the Bank Reconciliation Statement.
In Accounting, precision is paramount. The prescribed format is sacrosanct.
A business keeps a Bank Account in the General Ledger. The bank sends a Bank Statement.
These two must agree. When they do not, we reconcile. We find the truth.

Let us establish the rules.
The Bank Account in our ledger is an asset. Debit to increase. Credit to decrease.
The Bank Statement is from the bank's perspective. To the bank, our favorable balance is a liability. They owe us money. So, they credit it.
If we are overdrawn, it is an asset to the bank. They debit it.
Remember this inversion. It is where many students fail. You will not.

We have a problem to solve. The numbers never lie, and this bank reconciliation is about to confess everything.

## Subtopic: Drawing the Skeleton and the Bank Balance

[Prof Mokoena gestures as the skeleton of the statement appears.]

We begin by drawing the skeleton. The format first.
Heading: Bank Reconciliation Statement as at...
We will structure it with two columns: Debit and Credit.

Let us look at our given facts.
The bank statement shows a favorable balance of five thousand three hundred and twenty Rand.
Favorable on the bank statement. That means it is a credit balance. The bank owes us.
We write: "Credit balance as per Bank Statement."
We post the figure: R5 320 in the Credit column.

There. The first number is posted. Traceable and correct.
If you place a favorable bank statement balance on the debit side, you have failed before you have begun. The format is your guide.

## Subtopic: Outstanding Items

[Prof Mokoena's pace slows slightly, emphasising each item.]

Now we process the outstanding items. These are items in our books that the bank does not yet know about. We must update the statement to reflect reality.

First, an outstanding deposit of two thousand one hundred Rand.
When we deposit money, we debit our Bank Account in the ledger. It increases our asset.
The bank has not yet processed it. When they do, they will credit our account, because their liability to us increases.
Therefore, in the reconciliation statement, we must place it on the Credit side.
We write: "Credit outstanding deposit."
We post the figure: R2 100 in the Credit column.

Next, outstanding cheques. We issued them. We credited our Bank Account to decrease the asset.
The bank has not yet cashed them. When they do, they will debit our account to reduce their liability to us.
So, we must place them on the Debit side of the statement.
We write: "Debit outstanding cheques."
Cheque number 411 for one thousand four hundred and eighty Rand.
We post R1 480 in the Debit column.
Cheque number 415 for six hundred and fifty Rand.
We post R650 in the Debit column.

Every figure traceable. Every placement logical.

## Subtopic: Balancing and Conclusion

[Prof Mokoena points to the columns.]

The final step. We balance visibly.
We sum the Credit side. R5 320 plus R2 100. That gives us a total of seven thousand four hundred and twenty Rand. R7 420.
We sum the Debit side so far. R1 480 plus R650. That is two thousand one hundred and thirty Rand. R2 130.

The balancing figure is the difference. R7 420 minus R2 130 equals five thousand two hundred and ninety Rand.
This is the balance that must exist in our Bank Account in the General Ledger.
Since the Credit side is larger, our balancing figure must go to the Debit column to make both sides equal.
But wait. What does this mean for our ledger?
A balancing figure in the Debit column of the reconciliation means our Bank Account in the ledger has a Debit balance. A favorable asset balance.
We write: "Debit balance as per Bank Account."
We post the figure: R5 290 in the Debit column.

Both columns now total R7 420. The statement balances. The numbers agree.

Finally, an internal control point. Why do we do this?
Regular bank reconciliations prevent fraud and detect errors promptly. It is a non-negotiable control for any business.
Numbers never lie, provided you keep them honest.

I am Prof Mokoena. Goodbye.
