# Post-Session Q&A

### subtopic_2 — Drawing the Skeleton and the Bank Balance

**Assistant:** Students always ask this: the R5 290 sits in the debit column, so why call it a debit balance and not a credit?

**Tutor:** Because the reconciliation statement is not the ledger — it is a proof of what the ledger balance must be. The credit side totals R7 420: the favourable bank statement balance of R5 320 plus the outstanding deposit of R2 100. The debit side holds only the outstanding cheques, R1 480 plus R650, which is R2 130. The R5 290 we insert is the balancing figure — the amount of debit the picture is missing. And the only place that debit can live is the bank account in our ledger. A favourable bank account is an asset, assets carry debit balances, and the numbers never lie: the statement has just proved that debit balance to the cent.

### subtopic_3 — Outstanding Items

**Assistant:** Second one from the pre-test pile: does anyone still write cheques? Why are outstanding cheques even examinable in the EFT era?

**Tutor:** The cheque is the vehicle; the principle being examined is the timing difference, and timing differences did not retire with the chequebook. Record a payment at seven minutes to midnight on the last day of the month and it leaves your cash payments journal in March but reaches the bank statement in April. That is an outstanding payment, whatever instrument carried it. The examiner uses cheques because each one has a number and an amount that must be posted in the marked order — cheque 411 for R1 480, cheque 415 for R650, each on its own line. Master the entry, not the instrument, and the format earns its marks in any decade.

### subtopic_4 — Balancing and Conclusion

**Assistant:** And if the bank statement balance is overdrawn instead of favourable — where does the skeleton start then?

**Tutor:** Then the opening figure changes sides, and the reasoning must be said aloud: an overdraft means the bank has paid out money you did not have, so you owe the bank. On the bank's own books you are now a debtor — their asset — so the bank statement shows your balance as a debit. Your skeleton therefore opens with 'Debit balance as per bank statement' and the figure sits in the debit column. Every other rule stands unchanged: outstanding deposits still strengthen your side, outstanding cheques still reduce it, and the balancing figure still tells you, without argument, what the bank account in the ledger must show.

### subtopic_4 — Balancing and Conclusion

**Assistant:** Last one: is the balancing figure always the answer the question wants, or must the format itself be shown?

**Tutor:** Both, and this is where candidates hand back easy marks. The balancing figure is the destination, but the prescribed format is the journey the marker pays for: the correct heading with the date, each outstanding item on its own labelled line, the totals ruled and equal. Write only the final R5 290 and you have an answer worth one mark at most; present the full statement in the marked order and every line earns as you go. The discipline is the same as any ledger work — draw the skeleton first, post every figure in order, balance visibly. Do that and the reconciliation statement becomes among the most reliable marks in the paper.
