**Student:** I'm so confused about the balancing figure. Prof Mokoena said the total was R7 420, and the debit side only had R2 130. We put the R5 290 on the debit side to balance it. But then he said it's a *debit* balance in the ledger? Shouldn't a number in the debit column mean a credit balance?

**Mandy:** This is a classic trap! Think about it like a scale. The credit side was heavy—it weighed R7 420. The debit side was light—only R2 130. We *had* to put the R5 290 on the debit side just to make the scales balance. But that R5 290 represents the leftover weight that inherently belongs to the heavier side. Wait, no, scratch the scale analogy, let's use the actual Accounting rule.
The reconciliation statement is working out what the ledger balance *should* be. We know the total is R7 420. For the debit side to equal R7 420, we are "missing" R5 290 of debit entries. Therefore, our actual ledger account must have a debit balance of R5 290 to make this whole picture true. It's the balance brought down, effectively.

**Student:** Why do we even have outstanding cheques? Don't we just EFT everything now?

**Mandy:** Haha, true! In the real world, EFTs clear much faster. But in Accounting exams, cheques are still standard because they perfectly test timing differences. Even with EFTs, if you process a payment at 11:59 PM on the last day of the month, it might leave your books in March but only hit the bank statement in April. So the principle of an "outstanding payment" still applies!

**Student:** If the bank statement is overdrawn, do we start on the debit side?

**Mandy:** Exactly! If you are overdrawn, you owe the bank money. To the bank, you are now an asset (a debtor). Since assets are debits, an overdrawn balance will appear as a debit on your bank statement. So in your reconciliation skeleton, you'd start with "Debit balance as per Bank Statement" and put the figure in the debit column. You nailed it.
