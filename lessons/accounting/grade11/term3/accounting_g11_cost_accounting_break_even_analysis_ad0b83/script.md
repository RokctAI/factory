## Subtopic: The Spaza Shop Cash Box (Fixed vs Variable)

Hello, my child. Come, sit down. You know, people think accounting is just a bunch of difficult rules made up by people in suits. But it isn't. Your local spaza shop already does double-entry bookkeeping, even if you don't know it yet. It is just common sense written down neatly. Today, we're going to talk about a very important, real-world question: "Am I actually making money at the end of the month, or am I just working hard for free?"

In formal accounting language, we call this finding the **Break-even point**. But before we get to the big formal words and the scary formulas, let's look at the everyday cash box. Let's make it real.

Imagine you run a beautiful little spaza shop selling cool drinks. To keep the shop open and safe, you have certain expenses that you just cannot run away from. First of all, you have to pay rent for the container. Let's say that rent is a huge R60 000 a month. Whether you sell one cool drink, a hundred cool drinks, or ten thousand cool drinks, the landlord does not care. He still wants his full R60 000 on the first of the month. It doesn't change. Even if it rains for a week and no customers come, you must pay. We call this a **Fixed Cost**. Fixed costs are like a hungry, roaring monster in the corner of your shop that eats exactly the same amount of food every single month, no matter how hard you work. Other examples of fixed costs could be the salary of your permanent security guard, or the fixed monthly insurance premium you pay to protect your stock. They stay the same, regardless of how busy the shop is.

Now, to sell a cool drink, you first have to buy that cool drink from the big wholesaler in town. Let's say it costs you R30 to buy one single bottle. If you have a good day and you sell 10 bottles, it costs you R300 to buy them. If you sell zero bottles today, it costs you nothing, because you didn't have to buy any extra stock. This cost goes up and down directly depending on how much you sell. We call this a **Variable Cost**. It varies. Another variable cost might be the little plastic bags you give to customers. Sell more items, use more bags.

So, let us lock this in our minds before we move on. Fixed costs stay exactly the same no matter your sales volume. Variable costs change based on exactly how much stock you buy and sell. A very common mistake students make is confusing the two. Always ask yourself: "If I close the shop for a week, will this cost go down to zero?" If yes, it is variable. If no, it is fixed.

## Subtopic: Making a Profit on One Item (Contribution Margin)

Okay, so you took a taxi to town and you bought the cool drink for R30. Now, you are a smart business person, so you definitely don't bring it back and sell it for R30! You would be working for free! You put it on the shelf, make it nice and cold, and sell it to your customers for R50.

Let's do the simple math on that single transaction. The money going into your cash tin is R50. That is your **Selling Price**. The money you originally paid to the wholesaler is R30. That is your **Variable Cost**.

When you subtract the R30 variable cost from the R50 selling price, you are left with R20 in your hand.
R50 minus R30 equals R20.

Now, listen carefully, my child. A lot of students jump up and say, "Yay! I made R20 profit!" But this R20 is very special, and it is NOT your pure profit yet. Why? Because remember that hungry monster we talked about? The R60 000 rent! He is waiting in the corner.

This R20 is what each single cool drink *contributes* towards paying off that big rent monster. In Accounting, we call this the **Contribution Margin per unit**. The word "contribution" is perfect because it describes exactly what the money is doing. It is contributing to the fixed costs. It is the selling price per unit minus the variable cost per unit.

Every time you hear the bell ring and you sell one cool drink, you get R20 to put into the "Rent Money" jar. Sell another one, another R20 goes into the jar. Sell ten, that is R200 into the jar. We haven't taken any money home for ourselves yet. Every single R20 is just marching towards that R60 000 target. Only once the monster is fully fed can we start putting R20 notes into our own pockets. This is why understanding the contribution margin is the secret weapon of every good business owner.

## Subtopic: Calculating the Break-even Point

Now we come to the big question. The question that keeps business owners awake at night. How many cool drinks do you actually need to sell just to fill up that "Rent Money" jar? How many of those R20 notes do you need to stack up to make exactly R60 000?

If you make exactly R60 000 in contribution margin, you can finally pay the rent. At this exact moment, you won't make any extra profit, but—and this is the good news—you also won't make a loss. You will exactly break even. This magic moment is the **Break-even Point**. It is the target you must hit just to survive the month.

Let's do the calculation together using our everyday logic. We take the big hungry monster, which is the **Total Fixed Costs**, and we want to see how many R20 notes fit into it. So we divide it by the **Contribution Margin per unit**.

Our Total Fixed Costs are R60 000.
Our Contribution Margin per unit is R20.

We take R60 000 and divide it by R20. Let's work it out easily in our heads. Cross off the zeros at the end... so that becomes 6000 divided by 2. What is half of six thousand? That gives us 3 000.

You need to sell exactly 3 000 cool drinks this month. Let's think about what this number actually means.
If you have a terrible month and you only sell 2 999 cool drinks, you are R20 short on your rent. You are making a loss.
If you sell exactly 3 000 cool drinks, you have broken even. The rent is paid, but your own pocket is empty. Profit is zero.
But! The magic happens on the next sale. If you sell 3 001 cool drinks, that very next R20 doesn't go to the rent anymore. The rent is already paid! That R20 goes straight into your pocket. You finally made R20 pure profit! If you sell 3 002 cool drinks, you have R40 pure profit.

This is why we calculate the break-even point. It gives the business owner a clear, solid target. "I must sell 3 000 units before I can even think about buying myself a new pair of shoes."

## Subtopic: The Formal Exam Format

Now, you understand the cash box beautifully. You know how it works in the real world. The logic is exactly the same as our spaza shop, we just need to use the formal formula and lay it out properly. The marker cannot read your mind, they can only read your pen.

Here is the example problem exactly as you will see it in a test:
"A manufacturer has fixed costs of R60 000 per month. The selling price is R50 per unit and the variable cost is R30 per unit. Calculate the break-even point in units."

The formal formula for the Break-even point is:
**Total Fixed Costs / (Selling Price per unit - Variable Cost per unit)**

When you are sitting in that exam room, don't panic. Just write the formula down first. That is often your first mark!
Now, let's plug in our numbers from the example problem. We call this substitution.
Total Fixed Costs = R60 000
Selling Price per unit = R50
Variable Cost per unit = R30

Step 1: Calculate the bottom part of the formula, which is our contribution margin. Show the marker what you are doing.
R50 - R30 = R20

Step 2: Divide the Total Fixed Costs by the contribution margin you just calculated.
R60 000 / R20 = 3 000.

Now, here is a very common mistake where students throw away easy marks. Do not just write "3 000". 3 000 what? Rands? Elephants? Cool drinks? You must write **3 000 units**. If you write R3 000, the marker will mark it wrong, because break-even is about how many items you need to sell, not how much money it is.

Always show your working in the exam! Write the formula, show the substitution step clearly, calculate the contribution margin, and then write the final answer with the correct unit. The marker needs to see how your beautiful mind got there. If you make a small calculator error but your steps are right, you can still get part marks. If you just write a wrong final answer, you get zero.

## Subtopic: Ethics and Internal Control

Before we finish up for today, let's think about keeping our shop safe and sound. We calculated that you need to sell 3 000 units just to break even. That means every single unit counts! Every single cool drink is precious.

Imagine if a box of cool drinks is stolen from the shelf, or if an employee drops a crate and breaks them. What happens to our break-even point? You still paid the R30 variable cost to the wholesaler to buy them, but you will never, ever get the R50 selling price from a customer. That contribution margin is gone forever! This means you have to sell even more cool drinks just to make up for the loss and pay the rent! Theft and breakages push your break-even point higher and higher, making it much harder to survive.

To prevent this disaster, you must have strong internal controls. A vital internal control is doing regular physical stock counts. At the end of every week, you or a trusted manager must count the actual physical stock on the shelf and compare it to the stock records in the books. If the books say you should have 500 bottles, but you only count 450, you know you have a problem immediately, and you can investigate before it destroys your profit. You should also make sure the storage room is locked and that only one person has the key. We call that segregation of duties and access control.

Remember, my child, accounting isn't scary. It isn't just numbers on a page. It's just the spaza shop cash box, protected and written down neatly.
