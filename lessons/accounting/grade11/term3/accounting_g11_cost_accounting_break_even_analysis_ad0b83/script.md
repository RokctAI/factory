## Subtopic: The Spaza Shop Cash Box (Fixed vs Variable)
Hello, my child. I'm Aunty Grace. Your spaza shop already does double-entry bookkeeping, even if you don't know it yet. Today, we're going to talk about a very important question: "Am I actually making money, or am I just working for free?"

In accounting, we call this finding the **Break-even point**. But before we get to the big formal words, let's look at the cash box.

Imagine you run a little spaza shop selling cool drinks. To keep the shop open, you have to pay rent for the container. Let's say that rent is R60 000 a month. Whether you sell one cool drink or ten thousand cool drinks, the landlord still wants his R60 000. It doesn't change. We call this a **Fixed Cost**. Fixed costs are like a hungry monster that eats the same amount of food every month, no matter how hard you work.

Now, to sell a cool drink, you first have to buy it from the wholesaler. Let's say it costs you R30 to buy one bottle. If you sell 10 bottles, it costs you R300. If you sell zero bottles, it costs you nothing. This cost goes up and down depending on how much you sell. We call this a **Variable Cost**.

So, fixed costs stay the same. Variable costs change based on how much stock you buy and sell.

## Subtopic: Making a Profit on One Item (Contribution Margin)
Okay, so you bought the cool drink for R30. You are smart, so you don't sell it for R30! You put it on the shelf and sell it to your customers for R50.

Let's do the simple math. Money in the tin is R50. That is your **Selling Price**.
Money you paid to the wholesaler is R30. That is your **Variable Cost**.

When you subtract the R30 variable cost from the R50 selling price, you are left with R20.
R50 minus R30 equals R20.

This R20 is very special. It is not your pure profit yet. Why? Because remember that hungry monster? The R60 000 rent!

This R20 is what each cool drink *contributes* towards paying off the rent. In Accounting, we call this the **Contribution Margin per unit**. It is the selling price per unit minus the variable cost per unit.

Every time you sell one cool drink, you get R20 to put into the "Rent Money" jar.

## Subtopic: Calculating the Break-even Point
Now, here is the big question. How many cool drinks do you need to sell just to fill up that "Rent Money" jar? How many R20 notes do you need to make exactly R60 000?

If you make exactly R60 000, you can pay the rent. You won't make any extra profit, but you also won't make a loss. You will break even. This is the **Break-even Point**.

Let's do the calculation. We take the big monster, the **Total Fixed Costs**, and we divide it by the **Contribution Margin per unit**.

Our Total Fixed Costs are R60 000.
Our Contribution Margin per unit is R20.

R60 000 divided by R20. Let's work it out. Cross off the zeros... 6000 divided by 2. That gives us 3 000.

You need to sell 3 000 cool drinks this month. If you sell exactly 3 000 cool drinks, you have broken even. If you sell 2 999, you are making a loss. If you sell 3 001, you finally made R20 pure profit!

## Subtopic: The Formal Exam Format
Now, you understand the cash box. Let's look at how Prof Mokoena and the exam papers want to see this. The logic is exactly the same, we just use the formal formula.

The formula for the Break-even point is:
**Total Fixed Costs / (Selling Price per unit - Variable Cost per unit)**

Let's plug in our numbers from the example problem.
Total Fixed Costs = R60 000
Selling Price per unit = R50
Variable Cost per unit = R30

Step 1: Calculate the bottom part of the formula (the contribution margin).
R50 - R30 = R20

Step 2: Divide the Total Fixed Costs by the contribution margin.
R60 000 / R20 = 3 000 units.

Always show your working in the exam! Don't just write 3 000 units. Write the formula, show the substitution, and then the final answer. The marker needs to see how you got there.

## Subtopic: Ethics and Internal Control
Before we finish, let's think about keeping our shop safe. If you need to sell 3 000 units to break even, every single unit counts!

If stock is stolen from the shelf, you still paid the R30 variable cost, but you never got the R50 selling price. This means you have to sell even more cool drinks just to pay the rent!

To prevent this, you must do regular physical stock counts and compare the actual stock on the shelf to the stock records. This is a vital internal control.

Remember, my child, accounting isn't scary. It's just the spaza shop cash box, written down neatly. Keep practicing!
