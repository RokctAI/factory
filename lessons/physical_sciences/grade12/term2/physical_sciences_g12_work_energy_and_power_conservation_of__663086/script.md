## Subtopic: Introduction to Mechanical Energy

Welcome, Grade 12s. They call me the Science Queen. I teach Physical Sciences at Supacharge, and I demand nothing less than data-sheet precision. Today, we are conquering the Conservation of Mechanical Energy. Before we touch a single formula, we must establish our foundation.

You already know the two forms of mechanical energy. First, there is kinetic energy, denoted by a capital E with a subscript k. This is the energy an object possesses due to its motion. The formula from your data sheet is $E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$. If an object is moving, it has kinetic energy. If it is stationary, its velocity is zero, and therefore its kinetic energy is zero.

Second, we have gravitational potential energy, denoted by a capital E with a subscript p. This is the energy an object possesses due to its position relative to a reference point, usually the ground. The formula is $E_p = mgh$. If an object is at a height above the ground, it has gravitational potential energy. If it is on the ground, its height is zero, and its gravitational potential energy is zero.

Mechanical energy, which we will denote as $E_{mech}$, is simply the sum of these two energies. Therefore, $E_{mech} = E_k + E_p$. It is the total energy associated with the motion and position of an object. Understand this definition perfectly, because it is the key to everything that follows.

## Subtopic: The Principle of Conservation

Now we elevate our understanding to the principle itself. In Physical Sciences, principles govern the universe, and we state them precisely as required by the CAPS guidelines. The Principle of Conservation of Mechanical Energy states that in an isolated system, the total mechanical energy of an object remains constant.

What does "isolated system" mean? It means there are no external non-conservative forces doing work on our object. No applied forces, no tension, and crucially, no friction or air resistance. In the absence of these forces, the total mechanical energy is conserved. It does not disappear; it merely transforms.

Mathematically, this means the mechanical energy at any initial point is equal to the mechanical energy at any final point. We write this as $E_{mech, i} = E_{mech, f}$.
Expanding this using our definitions, we get:
$(E_k + E_p)_i = (E_k + E_p)_f$.
Or, fully expanded:
$\frac{1}{2}mv_i^2 + mgh_i = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 + mgh_f$.

This equation is your royal scepter. With it, you can solve for unknown velocities or heights simply by comparing two states of an object. The beauty of this principle is that the path taken does not matter—only the initial and final positions.

## Subtopic: Solving the Falling Ball Problem

Let us apply this principle to a classic scenario. Listen carefully to the problem: A 2 kg ball is dropped from a height of 5 m. Use conservation of mechanical energy to calculate its speed just before it strikes the ground. Ignore air resistance.

Step one is always to visualize the scenario and identify our two points. Point 1 is the initial state: the ball is at a height of 5 m. Because it is "dropped", its initial velocity is zero. Point 2 is the final state: the ball is just about to strike the ground, so its height is zero. We need to find its final velocity.

We begin by stating the principle mathematically. Do not skip this step, or you forfeit your first mark.
$(E_k + E_p)_i = (E_k + E_p)_f$

Now, we expand the formula exactly as it appears on your NSC data sheet.
$\frac{1}{2}mv_i^2 + mgh_i = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 + mgh_f$

Next comes the explicit substitution. This is where the mark sheet gives substitution marks, so we must be meticulous. We substitute the mass $m = 2 \text{ kg}$, the initial velocity $v_i = 0 \text{ m\cdot s}^{-1}$, the gravitational acceleration $g = 9,8 \text{ m\cdot s}^{-2}$, and the initial height $h_i = 5 \text{ m}$. For the final state, the mass remains 2 kg, the final velocity $v_f$ is our unknown, and the final height $h_f = 0 \text{ m}$.

Let us substitute:
$\frac{1}{2}(2)(0)^2 + (2)(9,8)(5) = \frac{1}{2}(2)v_f^2 + (2)(9,8)(0)$

Notice what happens. The initial kinetic energy term becomes zero because the initial velocity is zero. The final potential energy term becomes zero because the final height is zero. The equation simplifies elegantly:
$0 + 98 = v_f^2 + 0$
$98 = v_f^2$

## Subtopic: Final Calculation and Mark Sheet Advice

We are one step away from our regal finish. We have $v_f^2 = 98$. To find the final velocity, we take the square root of both sides.
$v_f = \sqrt{98}$

Calculating this gives us $v_f = 9,8994...$
As a rule in Physical Sciences, we round off our final answer to a minimum of two decimal places. Therefore, the speed is $9,90 \text{ m\cdot s}^{-1}$.

Do not forget the SI units! A number without a unit is meaningless in my classroom. The final answer is $9,90 \text{ m\cdot s}^{-1}$.

Let us review the mark allocation for this type of question. You receive one mark for the formula: $(E_k + E_p)_i = (E_k + E_p)_f$. You receive one mark for the correct substitution on the left side, and one mark for the correct substitution on the right side. Finally, you receive one mark for the correct answer with the correct unit. That is four marks secured through pure discipline.

Remember, Mechanical Energy is only conserved when there are no non-conservative forces doing work. If air resistance were present, we could not use this principle in this way. But when the system is isolated, the mathematics is absolute. Keep your substitution explicit, watch your units, and the marks will be yours. Science Queen, out.
