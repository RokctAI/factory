# Mandy Post-Session Q&A: Resistors in Parallel

**Student:** Why did Ms Dlamini say the total resistance is always smaller than the smallest resistor? That makes no sense.
**Mandy:** I know, right? It feels so weird. But remember the supermarket analogy! Even if you open a really slow checkout line (a high resistance resistor), it's still *an extra path*. Any extra path makes it slightly easier for the total crowd (current) to get through than before. So the overall resistance of the whole system drops.

**Student:** I keep getting the math wrong when adding the fractions.
**Mandy:** Fractions can be tricky. The safest way is to find the lowest common denominator first. In the example, we had 1/2, 1/3, and 1/6. The number 6 divides perfectly into all of them. So, change 1/2 into 3/6, and 1/3 into 2/6. Then you just add the top numbers: 3 + 2 + 1 = 6. So you get 6/6.

**Student:** Do I always have to use the formula exactly as it is on the data sheet? Can't I just use `Rp = (R1 * R2) / (R1 + R2)`?
**Mandy:** Great question. You *can* use that shortcut formula if you only have exactly two resistors in parallel. But Ms Dlamini insists on the `1/Rp` formula because (1) it's exactly what's on the CAPS data sheet, which guarantees you the formula mark, and (2) it works for three or more resistors, like the example we just did! The shortcut formula falls apart if you have three resistors. Play it safe for the exams and stick to the data sheet!

**Student:** I got 1 for my total resistance, so when I flipped it, it was still 1. Did I have to flip it?
**Mandy:** Yes! You always, always have to flip it. In this specific example, the math worked out to `1/Rp = 1`. The inverse of 1 is 1/1, which is still 1. So you got lucky! But if you got `1/Rp = 2`, and you forgot to flip it, you'd write the answer as 2 ohms. But the correct answer is `Rp = 1/2 = 0.5 ohms`. That's a huge difference! Don't rely on luck. Build the habit of flipping it every single time.
