## Subtopic: The Principle of Stoichiometry and the Balanced Equation

Specifically, we are looking at stoichiometry. In Physical Sciences, numbers never lie, provided you know exactly how to speak their language.

The physical principle here is the Law of Conservation of Mass. Matter cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction. Therefore, the total mass of the reactants must exactly equal the total mass of the products. We represent this reality using a balanced chemical equation. The coefficients—those large numbers in front of the formulas—tell us the exact molar ratio in which substances react and form. If the equation is not balanced, your foundation is cracked, and every calculation that follows will be mathematically incorrect.

Let us look at our exact problem. Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide produced when 8 grams of methane burns completely in excess oxygen.

First, we need the balanced equation for the combustion of methane. Methane is $\text{CH}_4$, and it reacts with oxygen gas, $\text{O}_2$. Complete combustion of a hydrocarbon always yields carbon dioxide, $\text{CO}_2$, and water, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$.

The unbalanced equation is:
$\text{CH}_4 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$

We have one carbon on the left and one on the right. Carbon is balanced.
We have four hydrogens on the left, but only two on the right. We place a coefficient of 2 in front of the water.
Now check the oxygen. We have two oxygens in the carbon dioxide and two in the water, giving us four oxygens on the right. To balance this, we place a coefficient of 2 in front of the oxygen gas on the left.

The balanced equation is:
$\text{CH}_4 + 2\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$

Do not skip this step. The balanced equation is your roadmap. The ratio of $\text{CH}_4$ to $\text{O}_2$ to $\text{CO}_2$ to $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ is 1 to 2 to 1 to 2.

## Subtopic: Moles to Moles: The Molar Ratio

Now that our equation is balanced, we must convert what we are given into moles. The balanced equation speaks in moles, not in grams. You cannot use a mass ratio directly.

We are given $8\text{ g}$ of methane, $\text{CH}_4$. We need to find the moles of methane. Look at your NSC data sheet. The formula is $n = \frac{m}{M}$.
$n$ is the number of moles, $m$ is the given mass in grams, and $M$ is the molar mass in grams per mole.

Let us calculate the molar mass of methane, $M(\text{CH}_4)$. From the periodic table:
Carbon is $12\text{ g\cdot mol}^{-1}$. Hydrogen is $1\text{ g\cdot mol}^{-1}$, and there are 4 of them.
$M(\text{CH}_4) = 12 + 4(1) = 16\text{ g\cdot mol}^{-1}$.

Now, substitute explicitly into the formula. This is where the mark sheet gives substitution marks. Do not just write the final answer.
$n(\text{CH}_4) = \frac{m}{M}$
$n(\text{CH}_4) = \frac{8}{16}$
$n(\text{CH}_4) = 0,5\text{ mol}$

We have $0,5\text{ mol}$ of methane. The problem asks for the mass of carbon dioxide, $\text{CO}_2$. We must cross the bridge from methane to carbon dioxide using the stoichiometric ratio from our balanced equation.

From the balanced equation:
$1\text{ mol of CH}_4$ produces $1\text{ mol of CO}_2$.
The ratio is 1:1.

Therefore, $0,5\text{ mol of CH}_4$ will produce $0,5\text{ mol of CO}_2$.
$n(\text{CO}_2) = 0,5\text{ mol}$.

Notice that we were told the oxygen is in excess. That phrasing is critical. It means methane is the limiting reagent; it dictates exactly how much product can form. We base all our calculations on the limiting reagent.

## Subtopic: Converting to Mass and Securing the Marks

We now know we have $0,5\text{ mol}$ of carbon dioxide. But the question did not ask for moles; it asked for mass. We must convert moles back to mass.

We use the same formula from the data sheet, $n = \frac{m}{M}$, rearranged to solve for mass:
$m = n \times M$.

First, we need the molar mass of carbon dioxide, $M(\text{CO}_2)$. Again, refer to the periodic table:
Carbon is $12\text{ g\cdot mol}^{-1}$. Oxygen is $16\text{ g\cdot mol}^{-1}$, and we have 2 of them.
$M(\text{CO}_2) = 12 + 2(16) = 12 + 32 = 44\text{ g\cdot mol}^{-1}$.

Now, we substitute our values. Show the substitution clearly.
$m(\text{CO}_2) = n \times M$
$m(\text{CO}_2) = 0,5 \times 44$
$m(\text{CO}_2) = 22$

Finally, we write the answer with the correct SI unit and sensible significant figures. Mass is measured in grams.
The mass of carbon dioxide produced is $22\text{ g}$.

Let us review the exam technique.
1. Balance the equation.
2. Convert the given mass to moles using $n = \frac{m}{M}$. Show substitution.
3. Use the molar ratio to find the moles of the unknown.
4. Convert the moles of the unknown back to the requested unit (mass) using $m = n \times M$.
5. State the final answer with correct SI units.

That is how you secure full marks in stoichiometry. Precision, correct formulas, and explicit substitution. Keep practicing.
