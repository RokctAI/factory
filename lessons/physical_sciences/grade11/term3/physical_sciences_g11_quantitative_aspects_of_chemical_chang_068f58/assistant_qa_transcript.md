**Student:** Mandy, I'm confused about finding the molar mass. When we balanced the equation, we put a 2 in front of the water (H2O). Does that mean I should multiply the molar mass of water by 2?

**Mandy:** This is such a common trap! No, you definitely don't multiply the molar mass by the big balancing number (the coefficient). The molar mass is strictly for *one* mole of the substance, just looking at its tiny numbers (subscripts) from the periodic table. Water (H2O) is always 18 g/mol, no matter if there's a 2, a 5, or a 10 in front of it in the equation! You only use the big numbers later when you look at the molar ratio.

**Student:** Oh, okay. So when *do* I use the big numbers?

**Mandy:** You use those big numbers only when you are "crossing the bridge" from the substance you know to the substance you want to find. Like when Ms Dlamini said "1 mole of CH4 produces 1 mole of CO2." The big numbers are just the recipe ratio telling you how many moles react and form.

**Student:** And why did she say we base everything on the "limiting reagent"? What if I used the oxygen instead of the methane?

**Mandy:** If you use the excess reagent (oxygen), you'll calculate way too much product! Think of making sandwiches. If you have 2 slices of bread (methane) and a whole jar of peanut butter (excess oxygen), you can still only make 1 sandwich. The bread limits you. The reaction stops when the limiting reagent runs out, so that's the one that decides how much product is actually made.
