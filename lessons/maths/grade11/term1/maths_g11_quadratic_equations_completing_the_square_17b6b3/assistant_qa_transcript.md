# Post-Session Q&A

### subtopic_1 — Introduction and Importance

**Assistant:** The question I hear most before tests: why complete the square at all when the quadratic formula always works?

**Tutor:** Two reasons, and both are worth marks. First, the quadratic formula is completing the square — apply the technique to the general equation ax² + bx + c = 0 and the formula falls out; know the derivation and you own the formula rather than rent it. Second, and decisive in an examination: when the instruction says 'solve by completing the square', the method is the question. Reach for the formula there and the answer may be right while the method marks — the majority of the marks — are forfeited. The examiner is paying for the technique, executed line by line. Deliver the technique.

### subtopic_2 — The Algorithm of Completing the Square

**Assistant:** Students constantly forget which number to add to both sides. Is there a reliable way to fix it in memory?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is the discipline of the algorithm itself: halve, then square. The coefficient of the x-term — in our equation x² + 6x + 2 = 0 that coefficient is 6 — is halved to give 3, and squared to give 9. Nine is the number added to both sides, and the left side folds into the perfect square (x + 3)². Note the precision: it is always the coefficient of x, never the constant, and it is added to both sides, because an equation is a balance and the marker checks that balance line by line. Halve, square, add to both sides — three verbs, executed in order, every time.

### subtopic_4 — The Critical Move

**Assistant:** Next: when the square root comes in, why must the plus-minus sign appear? Learners keep losing it.

**Tutor:** Because a square has two roots, and omitting one discards half the answer. If (x + 3)² = 7, then x + 3 is a number whose square is 7 — and both √7 and −√7 satisfy that condition, since a negative multiplied by itself is positive. The moment the square root is taken, write ± in the same movement: x + 3 = ±√7. This is not decoration; it is the line that splits one equation into two solutions, and the memorandum awards it specifically. Lose the sign and you lose a root, and with it the accuracy mark that root carried. In this method, the ± is the critical move.

### subtopic_5 — Factorising and Solving

**Assistant:** Last one from the pile: what changes when the coefficient of x squared is not one — say 2x² + 8x − 4 = 0?

**Tutor:** One additional opening step, executed before anything else: divide the entire equation by that leading coefficient. Two x squared plus 8x minus 4 equals nought becomes x² + 4x − 2 = 0 — every term divided, including the constant, because a partial division is an algebraic error the marker will find. From that point the algorithm proceeds unchanged: halve the 4 to get 2, square to get 4, add 4 to both sides, fold into (x + 2)² = 6, apply the ± root, isolate x. The distinction-level habit is stating the division as its own justified line — that line carries a method mark, and at the top grades every line must earn its keep.
