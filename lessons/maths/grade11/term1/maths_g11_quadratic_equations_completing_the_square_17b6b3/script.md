# Maths Grade 11: Quadratic Equations - Completing the square

## Subtopic: Introduction and Importance

Call me the Algebra Grandmaster. I teach Maths at Supacharge. Here's what I do to quadratic equations.

If you want to excel, you must understand the machinery behind the equations. We are looking at quadratic equations. You already know how to expand brackets and factorise perfect squares. That is basic. Today, we elevate that knowledge.

Why do we complete the square? Because not every quadratic equation can be factorised neatly. When the examiner gives you an equation that refuses to factorise, they are testing your algebraic precision. Completing the square is the technique that forces the equation into a perfect square format, allowing us to isolate the variable and solve it. It is the bridge to the quadratic formula.

## Subtopic: The Algorithm of Completing the Square

Precision is key. Here is the algorithm.

Step 1: Ensure the coefficient of \(x^2\) is 1. If it is not, you divide the entire equation by that coefficient.
Step 2: Move the constant term to the right side of the equation.
Step 3: This is the critical move. Take the coefficient of \(x\), halve it, square the result, and add it to *both* sides of the equation. This maintains equality while forcing a perfect square on the left.
Step 4: Factorise the perfect square on the left side.
Step 5: Solve for \(x\) by taking the square root of both sides, remembering to include both the positive and negative roots.

This is the standard approach. Examiners look for these specific steps to award method marks.

## Subtopic: Applying the Method

Let us apply this to a distinction-level problem.

Consider the equation: \(x^2 + 6x + 2 = 0\).

Attempt to factorise this. You will find no factors of 2 that add up to 6. We must complete the square.

Step 1: The coefficient of \(x^2\) is already 1. We proceed.
Step 2: Move the constant term, positive 2, to the right side.
\(x^2 + 6x = -2\).
This line earns the first method mark.

## Subtopic: The Critical Move

Step 3: The coefficient of \(x\) is positive 6.
Halve it: \(\frac{6}{2} = 3\).
Square it: \(3^2 = 9\).
Add 9 to *both* sides of the equation.
\(x^2 + 6x + 9 = -2 + 9\).
Simplify the right side:
\(x^2 + 6x + 9 = 7\).

This step is non-negotiable. It demonstrates your understanding of manipulating equations without altering their fundamental truth.

## Subtopic: Factorising and Solving

Step 4: The left side, \(x^2 + 6x + 9\), is now a perfect square. We factorise it.
\((x + 3)^2 = 7\).
This is the second method mark. You have successfully restructured the equation.

Step 5: Solve for \(x\). Take the square root of both sides.
\(x + 3 = \pm \sqrt{7}\).
Do not forget the plus-minus symbol. A quadratic equation typically has two solutions. Forgetting it will cost you accuracy marks.

Finally, isolate \(x\):
\(x = -3 \pm \sqrt{7}\).

Therefore, the two solutions are \(x = -3 + \sqrt{7}\) and \(x = -3 - \sqrt{7}\).

This is distinction-level thinking. Formula first, then precise application. Every step justified.
