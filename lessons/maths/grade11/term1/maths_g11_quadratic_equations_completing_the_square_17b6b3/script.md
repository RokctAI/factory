# Maths Grade 11: Quadratic Equations - Completing the square

## Subtopic: Introduction and Importance

Here's what I do to quadratic equations.

If you want to excel, you must understand the machinery behind the equations. We are looking at quadratic equations. You already know how to expand brackets and factorise perfect squares. That is basic. You have been taught to seek out factors, to test combinations of numbers until the expression falls apart into two neat parentheses. But what happens when the numbers refuse to cooperate? What happens when the expression does not neatly factorise? Today, we elevate that knowledge.

Why do we complete the square? Because not every quadratic equation can be factorised neatly. In fact, most quadratics in the real world, and indeed in the more challenging sections of your examinations, will resist simple factorisation. The coefficients will not align to produce integer factors. The roots might be irrational numbers, involving surds. When the examiner gives you an equation that refuses to factorise, they are testing your algebraic precision. They are not merely testing if you can spot a pattern; they are testing if you have the discipline to execute an algorithm. They want to see if you can take an awkward, uncompromising expression and bend it to your will through pure mathematical rigor.

Completing the square is the technique that forces the equation into a perfect square format, allowing us to isolate the variable and solve it. By applying a deliberate sequence of operations, we manipulate an otherwise insolvable trinomial into a form that yields to a simple square root. Furthermore, this is the very foundation upon which the quadratic formula is built. Before you even think about memorizing that formula—the familiar x equals negative b plus or minus the square root of b squared minus four a c all over two a—you must respect the derivation that birthed it. And that derivation is completing the square.

This is a fundamental skill. It is not a trick. It is a rigorous mathematical process. If you can master this, you master quadratic equations in their entirety. Let us delve into the pure mechanics of the method.

## Subtopic: The Algorithm of Completing the Square

Precision is key. Here is the algorithm. You must memorize this sequence. You must execute it without hesitation. Every step has a specific purpose, a specific structural goal.

Step 1: Ensure the coefficient of \(x^2\) is 1. If it is not, you divide the entire equation by that coefficient. For instance, if you are faced with \(3x^2 + 12x - 5 = 0\), you must divide every single term by 3. You would get \(x^2 + 4x - \frac{5}{3} = 0\). Do not neglect the constant, and do not neglect the zero on the other side. This normalisation is the critical first requirement. Without a leading coefficient of 1, the subsequent steps will fail catastrophically. The geometry of a perfect square trinomial demands that the leading term is a pure \(x^2\).

Step 2: Move the constant term to the right side of the equation. We want the variable terms isolated on the left. The \(x^2\) and \(x\) terms belong together; the constant belongs across the equals sign. We are clearing the workspace, so to speak, preparing a blank space to inject our completing value.

Step 3: This is the critical move. Pay absolute attention here. Take the coefficient of \(x\), halve it, square the result, and add it to *both* sides of the equation. Why both sides? Because an equation is a balance. What you do to the left, you must mirror exactly on the right. This maintains equality while forcing a perfect square on the left. The halving prepares the middle term of the future binomial, and the squaring completes the trinomial.

Step 4: Factorise the perfect square on the left side. Because of our precise manipulation in Step 3, the left side is mathematically guaranteed to be a perfect square trinomial. It will instantly collapse into a binomial squared. There is no guessing here. The binomial will always be \(x\) plus or minus the halved coefficient we found in Step 3. The sign in the middle matches the sign of the \(x\) coefficient.

Step 5: Solve for \(x\) by taking the square root of both sides, remembering to include both the positive and negative roots. The square root of \(k^2\) yields two possibilities: positive \(k\) and negative \(k\). Students who forget the plus-minus symbol immediately forfeit accuracy marks. Do not make that amateur mistake. When you un-square a variable expression, you acknowledge that both a positive and negative root could have produced that squared magnitude.

This is the standard approach. Examiners look for these specific steps to award method marks. If you deviate, if you skip steps, you invite error. Stick to the algorithm.

## Subtopic: Applying the Method

Let us apply this to a distinction-level problem. We will proceed logically, executing the algorithm precisely as defined, step by step, line by line.

Consider the equation: \(x^2 + 6x + 2 = 0\).

Attempt to factorise this first. You look for factors of positive 2 that sum to positive 6. The only integer factors of 2 are 1 and 2, or -1 and -2. Their sums are 3 and -3 respectively. Neither of these is 6. The traditional factorisation method has failed us. The numbers are stubborn. We must therefore complete the square. We will force a solution where guessing cannot find one.

Step 1: Inspect the coefficient of \(x^2\). The coefficient of \(x^2\) is already 1. There is an invisible '1' standing right in front of the \(x^2\). We do not need to divide the equation by anything. The normalisation condition is met. We can proceed immediately to the next phase of the algorithm.

Step 2: Move the constant term, positive 2, to the right side. How do we do this mathematically? We subtract 2 from both sides of the equality.

\(x^2 + 6x = -2\).

Observe the structure carefully. The variables are isolated on the left-hand side. The constant has been relocated to the right. This line earns the first method mark. It demonstrates intent. You are preparing the stage for the completion. You have recognised that the current constant is useless to us, and you have moved it out of the way to make room for the correct completing value.

Many students stumble here by bringing the \(x\) term across as well, or by forgetting to flip the sign of the constant. When positive 2 crosses the equality, it becomes negative 2. Algebraic discipline is non-negotiable.

## Subtopic: The Critical Move

Now, we execute Step 3. This is where the magic happens. This is the heart of the completing the square algorithm.

Step 3: The coefficient of \(x\) is positive 6. This is our target number. We must operate on it.
Halve it: \(\frac{6}{2} = 3\). The number 3 will be vital shortly. Do not lose track of it.
Square it: \(3^2 = 9\).

This 9 is the precise quantity required to complete the square on the left-hand side. It is the missing piece of the puzzle. We must add 9 to the equation. But remember the golden rule of algebra: balance. We add 9 to *both* sides of the equation.

\(x^2 + 6x + 9 = -2 + 9\).

Let us pause and examine this. The left side, \(x^2 + 6x + 9\), is now a mathematically engineered perfect square trinomial. We built it purposefully. The right side is a simple arithmetic sum. Let us simplify the right side by adding negative 2 and 9:

\(x^2 + 6x + 9 = 7\).

This step is absolutely non-negotiable. It demonstrates your profound understanding of manipulating equations without altering their fundamental truth.

A common mistake here is to add 9 to the left but forget the right. That destroys the equality. Another common error is to square the halved coefficient incorrectly. For instance, if the coefficient of \(x\) was negative, say -6, halving it gives -3. Squaring -3 still gives a positive 9. You always add a positive value to both sides to complete the square. Negative squares do not exist in real numbers.

## Subtopic: Factorising and Solving

We are now ready for the final descent. The heavy lifting is done. Now we reap the rewards of our precision.

Step 4: The left side, \(x^2 + 6x + 9\), is now a perfect square. We factorise it. Because we engineered it perfectly, we know it will form a binomial squared. The terms of the binomial are \(x\), and the halved coefficient we found earlier, which was positive 3.

\((x + 3)^2 = 7\).

Look at the elegance of that structure. We have compressed a sprawling trinomial into a single, compact squared bracket. This is the second method mark. You have successfully restructured the equation. If you expanded \((x + 3)^2\), you would get exactly \(x^2 + 6x + 9\).

Step 5: Solve for \(x\). The variable \(x\) is currently trapped inside the squared bracket. To isolate it completely, we must undo the square operation. We do this by taking the square root of both sides.

\(\sqrt{(x + 3)^2} = \pm \sqrt{7}\).

Which simplifies cleanly to:
\(x + 3 = \pm \sqrt{7}\).

I must repeat my earlier warning: do not forget the plus-minus symbol. A quadratic equation typically has two solutions. Forgetting the plus-minus symbol means you throw away half of your answer. It will cost you accuracy marks. Never take a square root in algebra without considering both roots.

Finally, we isolate \(x\) by moving the positive 3 to the right side, which makes it negative 3.

\(x = -3 \pm \sqrt{7}\).

This notation represents two distinct, separate roots. Let us write them out explicitly.

Therefore, the two solutions are:
\(x = -3 + \sqrt{7}\)
and
\(x = -3 - \sqrt{7}\).

These are irrational numbers. Leaving them in exact surd form is the most precise and accurate representation possible.

This is distinction-level thinking. Formula first, then precise application. Every step justified. You do not guess; you follow the algorithm.
