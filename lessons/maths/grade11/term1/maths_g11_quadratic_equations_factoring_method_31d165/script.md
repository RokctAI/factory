Welcome, student. Today, we turn our attention to one of the cornerstones of algebra: quadratic equations. More specifically, we will master the factoring method.

## Subtopic: Review and Introduction

Before we introduce the quadratic equation, let us briefly review algebraic expressions. You are already familiar with basic algebra and multiplication. If you have an expression such as three multiplied by the quantity x plus two, you know how to expand it. You distribute the three, yielding three x plus six. Similarly, if you multiply two binomials together, such as x plus one and x plus two, you apply the distributive property across all terms, often known as the FOIL method. The result is x squared plus three x plus two.

What we will be doing today is essentially the reverse of that process. When we factor a quadratic equation, we are taking an expanded expression—like x squared plus three x plus two—and breaking it down into its constituent multiplicative parts, or factors.

Why do we do this? In mathematics, factoring is a powerful analytical tool. By decomposing a complex quadratic expression into simpler linear binomials, we can easily find the values of x that make the entire equation equal to zero. These values are known as the roots, or solutions, of the equation.

Let us define exactly what a quadratic equation is. A quadratic equation is a second-degree polynomial equation. This means the highest power of the unknown variable, typically x, is two. The general, or standard form of a quadratic equation is a x squared plus b x plus c equals zero. It is imperative that the equation is set to zero; if it is not, your first maneuver must always be to rearrange the terms algebraically until it is.

In our standard form, a, b, and c represent known constants, and a cannot be zero. If a were zero, the x squared term would vanish, and the equation would reduce to a linear one.

Today, we will analyze a specific case study to illuminate the factoring method. Our example problem is: two x squared plus five x plus three equals zero. Let us proceed to deconstruct it.

## Subtopic: Standard Form and Identifying Coefficients

Observe our equation carefully: two x squared plus five x plus three equals zero.

Your first priority is always to verify that the equation is in standard form. Are the terms arranged in descending order of their exponents? Yes. The x squared term is first, followed by the x term, followed by the constant. Is the entire expression set equal to zero? Yes. We may proceed.

Our next crucial step is to identify the coefficients a, b, and c. We match our equation, two x squared plus five x plus three equals zero, against the standard form, a x squared plus b x plus c equals zero.

By direct comparison:
The coefficient of the squared term, a, is two.
The coefficient of the linear term, b, is five.
The constant term, c, is three.

A common pitfall is to ignore the signs of these coefficients. In our example, all terms are positive, making it straightforward. However, if our equation were two x squared minus five x minus three equals zero, then b would be negative five and c would be negative three. Always attach the sign to the coefficient.

Now that we have isolated a, b, and c, we will employ a technique known as factoring by grouping. To execute this, we must find two distinct numbers. Let us refer to them conceptually as p and q.

These two numbers, p and q, must satisfy two strict conditions simultaneously.
First, their product—p multiplied by q—must equal the product of our coefficients a and c.
Second, their sum—p plus q—must equal our middle coefficient b.

Let us calculate the target product, a multiplied by c. Since a is two and c is three, their product is six.
Our target sum is simply b, which is five.

We are therefore searching for two numbers that multiply to give six, and add together to give five. Let us systematically examine the factors of six.
The factors of six are one and six, and two and three. Since our target product is positive six and our target sum is positive five, we only need to consider positive factors.

Let us test the first pair: one and six. One multiplied by six is six. However, one plus six is seven. This does not match our target sum of five. Therefore, one and six are incorrect.

Let us test the second pair: two and three. Two multiplied by three is six. Two plus three is five. This is our match. The numbers we seek are two and three.

## Subtopic: Factoring by Grouping

Now that we have discovered our two numbers, two and three, what is their purpose? We will use them to split the middle term of our original quadratic equation.

Recall our equation: two x squared plus five x plus three equals zero.
We will take the middle term, five x, and rewrite it as the sum of two x and three x.
The equation now reads: two x squared plus two x plus three x plus three equals zero.

Notice that we have not altered the mathematical value of the equation; we have simply expanded its middle term. We now have an expression with four terms. This structure allows us to factor by grouping.

We will divide the four terms into two distinct pairs, or groups.
The first group consists of the first two terms: two x squared plus two x.
The second group consists of the final two terms: three x plus three.

Let us examine the first group: two x squared plus two x. What is the greatest common factor shared by both of these terms? Both terms contain a two, and both terms contain an x. Therefore, the greatest common factor is two x.
If we factor out two x, what remains? Dividing two x squared by two x leaves x. Dividing two x by two x leaves one.
Thus, the first group factors into: two x, multiplied by the quantity x plus one.

Now, let us examine the second group: three x plus three. The greatest common factor here is a positive three.
If we factor out positive three, we divide three x by three, leaving x, and we divide three by three, leaving one.
Thus, the second group factors into: positive three, multiplied by the quantity x plus one.

Let us recombine our equation. It now reads: two x multiplied by the quantity x plus one, plus three multiplied by the quantity x plus one, equals zero.

Observe the equation closely. A pattern has emerged. Both halves of the equation contain an identical binomial factor: x plus one. This is not a coincidence; it is the hallmark of correct factoring by grouping. If the binomials do not match, you have made a calculation error and must re-evaluate your steps.

Because x plus one is common to both major parts of our expression, we can factor it out of the entire equation.
If we factor out x plus one, what remains from the first part? Two x. What remains from the second part? Positive three.
Therefore, our expression factors completely into two binomials: the quantity x plus one, multiplied by the quantity two x plus three.
Our fully factored equation is: the quantity x plus one, multiplied by the quantity two x plus three, equals zero.

## Subtopic: The Zero-Product Property

We have successfully factored the quadratic expression. We transformed two x squared plus five x plus three into the product of x plus one and two x plus three. Now, we must solve for x. To accomplish this, we will invoke a fundamental mathematical axiom: the zero-product property.

The zero-product property states a simple but absolute truth: if the product of two or more quantities equals zero, then at least one of those quantities must be equal to zero.

If I tell you that A multiplied by B equals zero, logic dictates that either A equals zero, B equals zero, or both equal zero. There is no other way to multiply two numbers and arrive at zero.

We apply this property directly to our factored equation. We have the quantity x plus one, multiplied by the quantity two x plus three, set equal to zero.
According to the zero-product property, either the first factor equals zero, or the second factor equals zero.

We set up two separate, linear equations.
Equation one: x plus one equals zero.
Equation two: two x plus three equals zero.

These are elementary linear equations that you are fully equipped to solve.
Let us solve the first equation: x plus one equals zero. To isolate x, we subtract one from both sides. This yields our first solution: x equals negative one.

Now, let us solve the second equation: two x plus three equals zero.
First, we subtract three from both sides, which gives us two x equals negative three.
Next, we divide both sides by two. This yields our second solution: x equals negative three divided by two, or negative one point five.

We have found two solutions for our quadratic equation: x equals negative one, and x equals negative three halves.

In mathematics, a solution is only valid if it satisfies the original premise. As a final act of rigor, we should verify our answers.
Let us substitute x equals negative one back into the original equation: two x squared plus five x plus three equals zero.
Substituting negative one for x gives us: two multiplied by negative one squared, plus five multiplied by negative one, plus three.
Negative one squared is one, so two multiplied by one is two.
Five multiplied by negative one is negative five.
The expression becomes: two minus five plus three.
Two minus five is negative three. Negative three plus three is exactly zero. Our first solution is verified.

I encourage you to perform the same substitution for x equals negative three halves to prove its validity to yourself.

We have systematically deconstructed a quadratic equation, isolated its coefficients, executed factoring by grouping, and applied the zero-product property to extract the true values of x. Mastery of this process is non-negotiable for higher mathematics. Practice this method until it becomes second nature.
