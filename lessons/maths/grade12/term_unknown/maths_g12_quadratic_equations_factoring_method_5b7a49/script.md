# Tutor Script: Quadratic Equations - Factoring Method

## Subtopic: The Architecture of the Quadratic Equation

Welcome. I am the Algebra Grandmaster. Today, we dissect the quadratic equation. We are going to master the factoring method. Precision and structure are non-negotiable. If you want distinction-level marks, you must understand the architecture of what you are solving.

A quadratic equation in standard form is written as ax squared plus bx plus c equals zero. The variable x represents the unknown. The coefficients a, b, and c are constants, and a cannot be zero. If a were zero, the equation would be linear, not quadratic.

Observe our example for today: 2x squared plus 5x plus 3 equals zero.
Is it in standard form? Yes. The terms are in descending order of degree. The right-hand side is identically zero. This is your first checkpoint in an exam. Never attempt to factorise an equation unless it is equated to zero.

Let us identify our coefficients.
The leading coefficient, a, is 2.
The linear coefficient, b, is 5.
The constant term, c, is 3.

Notice that our leading coefficient is not 1. This classifies our expression as a non-monic quadratic trinomial. The approach required here is more rigorous than simple inspection. We will employ the product-sum method, often referred to as the AC method, to deconstruct the middle term.

## Subtopic: The AC Method for Non-Monic Quadratics

We proceed to the AC method. The objective is to split the middle term, the 5x, into two distinct terms. This will allow us to factorise by grouping.

First, we calculate the product of a and c.
Our a is 2. Our c is 3.
The product, ac, is 2 multiplied by 3, which equals 6.

Next, we look at our b value, the sum.
Our b is 5.

We require two integers. Let us call them p and q.
These integers must satisfy two conditions simultaneously.
Condition one: their product must equal ac. So, p times q equals 6.
Condition two: their sum must equal b. So, p plus q equals 5.

Let us evaluate the factor pairs of 6.
1 and 6. The product is 6, but the sum is 7. This fails the second condition.
2 and 3. The product is 6. The sum is 2 plus 3, which equals 5. This satisfies both conditions.

Our two integers are 2 and 3.

We now rewrite the original equation. We replace the linear term, 5x, with 2x plus 3x.
The equation becomes: 2x squared plus 2x plus 3x plus 3 equals zero.
Notice that the value of the equation has not changed. 2x plus 3x is still 5x. We have merely restructured it to enable the next analytical step. This line earns the method mark for correct substitution.

## Subtopic: Factorisation by Grouping

With four terms now established, we execute factorisation by grouping.
We divide the four terms into two pairs.
The first pair is 2x squared plus 2x.
The second pair is 3x plus 3.

Examine the first pair. What is the highest common factor?
Between 2x squared and 2x, the highest common factor is 2x.
We factor out the 2x.
2x squared divided by 2x leaves x. 2x divided by 2x leaves positive 1.
So, the first pair factors as: 2x, open bracket, x plus 1, close bracket.

Now, examine the second pair: 3x plus 3.
The highest common factor is 3.
We factor out the positive 3.
3x divided by 3 leaves x. 3 divided by 3 leaves positive 1.
The second pair factors as: positive 3, open bracket, x plus 1, close bracket.

Our equation now reads: 2x times (x plus 1) plus 3 times (x plus 1) equals zero.
Observe the binomial inside the brackets. It is x plus 1 in both cases. This is your internal verification. If the brackets do not match identically, you have made an arithmetic error in the previous step.

Since the binomial (x plus 1) is common to both terms, we can factor it out as a highest common factor of the entire expression.
We extract (x plus 1). What remains is the 2x from the first term and the positive 3 from the second term.
The fully factorised form is: (x plus 1) multiplied by (2x plus 3) equals zero.

This step is critical. You have transformed a sum of terms into a product of factors. This sets the stage for the final solution.

## Subtopic: The Zero Product Property

We have arrived at the final phase. We have the product of two binomials equal to zero: (x plus 1) multiplied by (2x plus 3) equals zero.

To solve for x, we apply the Zero Product Property. This theorem states that if the product of two real numbers is zero, then at least one of those numbers must be zero. Mathematically, if A times B equals zero, then A equals zero, or B equals zero, or both.

Applying this to our equation, we set each factor to zero independently.
Case 1: x plus 1 equals zero.
Case 2: 2x plus 3 equals zero.

We solve these linear equations.
For Case 1: subtract 1 from both sides. x equals negative 1.
For Case 2: subtract 3 from both sides, giving 2x equals negative 3. Then divide by 2. x equals negative 3 over 2.

Our final solutions are x equals negative 1, or x equals negative 3 over 2.
In an exam, state both solutions clearly. They represent the x-intercepts of the corresponding quadratic parabola.

Remember the distinction-level takeaway: standard form first, methodical AC method for non-monic quadratics, verify your grouping brackets match, and apply the Zero Product Property explicitly. Master this process, and quadratic equations will yield to you every time.
