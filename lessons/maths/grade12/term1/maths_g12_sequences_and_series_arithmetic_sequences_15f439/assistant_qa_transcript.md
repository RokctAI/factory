# Post-Session Q&A

### subtopic_1 — Introduction to Arithmetic Sequences and the General Formula

**Assistant:** The most common pre-test question: learners keep getting the wrong sign for d. Which way must the subtraction run?

**Tutor:** The definition settles it: the common difference is any term minus its predecessor — T₂ − T₁, T₃ − T₂, never the reverse. In our sequence 3; 7; 11 that gives 7 − 3 = 4, positive, and the sequence climbs. In a descending sequence such as 10; 8; 6 the same subtraction gives −2, and that negative sign is not an inconvenience — it is the answer. Reverse the subtraction and every subsequent line of working inherits the error, and the memorandum stops paying at the first wrong sign. State the definition, perform the subtraction in the defined order, and d takes care of itself.

### subtopic_1 — Introduction to Arithmetic Sequences and the General Formula

**Assistant:** Second: why does the general term use n minus one? Learners want to know why not simply n.

**Tutor:** Count the steps, not the terms. The first term a is the starting position — zero steps taken. Reaching the second term requires one step of size d; the third term, two steps; the twentieth term, nineteen steps. The number of steps is always one fewer than the position, hence Tₙ = a + (n − 1)d. Test it at the boundary, as an examiner would: substitute n = 1 and the formula returns a exactly, which is precisely what the first term must be. A formula that survives its boundary case is a formula you can trust in question seven — and deriving that check aloud is distinction-level habit.

### subtopic_2 — Solving the Example Problem

**Assistant:** Third: a learner solved for n and obtained four point five. Is that acceptable?

**Tutor:** It is not, and recognising why is worth a mark on its own. The symbol n is a position — first, second, twentieth — and positions are natural numbers; there is no four-and-a-halfth term. A fractional or negative n admits exactly two explanations: an arithmetic slip in the working, or the value being tested does not belong to the sequence — and examiners set both deliberately. Compare our worked example: T₂₀ = 3 + (20 − 1) × 4 = 3 + 76 = 79, where n = 20 entered as a whole number and the answer followed cleanly. When n emerges broken, halt, state the conclusion formally — 'n is not a natural number, therefore the value is not a term of the sequence' — and collect the reasoning mark.

### subtopic_3 — Exam Technique and Distinction-Level Takeaway

**Assistant:** Last one: when exactly is the general formula the correct tool, and when is it not?

**Tutor:** Deploy Tₙ = a + (n − 1)d the moment two conditions hold: the sequence is arithmetic — given, or proved by a constant difference — and the question involves any one of the four quantities Tₙ, a, n or d. The distinction-level insight is that the formula is one equation in four unknowns: given any three, it surrenders the fourth. Find the term, find the position, find the first term, find the difference — all the same substitution executed in a different direction. What it does not do is add terms; the moment a question asks for a sum, you have left this formula's jurisdiction. Know the boundary, and the sequences question becomes reliable marks.
