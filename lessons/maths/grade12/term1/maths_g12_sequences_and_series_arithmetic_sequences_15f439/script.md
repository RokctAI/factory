# Lesson Script: Arithmetic Sequences (Grade 12)
Tutor: Grandmaster

## Subtopic: Introduction to Arithmetic Sequences and the General Formula

Welcome. Call me the Algebra Grandmaster. I teach Maths at Supacharge. Today, we address Grade 12 Sequences and Series, specifically arithmetic sequences.

You are already familiar with the concept of linear number patterns from Grade 11. Mathematics is about recognizing fundamental patterns, and an arithmetic sequence is precisely that: it is a sequence of numbers in which the difference between any two consecutive terms is a constant. We refer to this as the constant difference, and we denote it by the letter $d$. In any such sequence, the first term is of primary importance, and it is denoted by $a$.

If you want to top the class and achieve distinction-level marks, you must always start with the underlying formula. The general term of an arithmetic sequence is a foundational building block for much of what follows in Grade 12, and it is given by:

$T_n = a + (n - 1)d$

Memorise this. This formula is your primary tool, your starting point for any question involving arithmetic sequences. Let us unpack the variables so there is no ambiguity. Here, $T_n$ represents the actual value of the $n$-th term. The variable $n$ is the position or the index of the term in the sequence. It is critically important to understand that $n$ must be a natural number ($n \in \mathbb{N}$). You cannot have a term sitting in the "two-and-a-half" position, nor can you have a term in the "negative-fifth" position. Term positions are strictly counting numbers: $1, 2, 3, 4$, and so on.

The variable $a$ is the first term, which is synonymous with $T_1$. The variable $d$ is the constant difference. Do not make the amateur mistake of calculating $d$ by subtracting the second term from the first. It must always be the subsequent term minus the preceding term. We calculate it as $T_2 - T_1$, or more generally, $T_k - T_{k-1}$ for any integer $k > 1$. If you reverse this order, your constant difference will have the wrong sign, and the rest of your calculations will unravel. Always use the structure: right-hand term minus left-hand term.

Examiners expect you to explicitly state the formula before substituting any values into it. Writing the correct formula on the page often secures your first method mark. Do not skip steps, and do not attempt to hold these variables entirely in your head. We use precise technical notation throughout our work because it eliminates errors and communicates clear logic.

When you look at an arithmetic sequence, your immediate objective is to identify $a$ and calculate $d$. Once you have isolated these two parameters, the sequence is completely defined. It is locked in. With $a$ and $d$ known, any term can be found, and the structure of the entire sequence is at your command.

Let us proceed to apply this formula and this structured thinking to a typical examination question.

## Subtopic: Solving the Example Problem

Consider the following example problem: Find the 20th term of the sequence 3; 7; 11; ...

Before we launch into calculations, we must confirm our foundations. First, verify that the sequence is indeed arithmetic. Never assume a sequence is arithmetic simply because it looks like one; prove it. We calculate the difference between consecutive terms.

$T_2 - T_1 = 7 - 3 = 4$
$T_3 - T_2 = 11 - 7 = 4$

The difference is constant. Therefore, we have rigorously established that it is an arithmetic sequence with a constant difference of $d = 4$.

The first term is clearly 3. Therefore, $a = 3$.

We now have the two defining parameters of our sequence. We require the 20th term, which means we are looking for the value of $T_{20}$. In this specific case, the position $n = 20$.

We state our formula. As I mentioned earlier, this line earns the method mark for selecting the correct mathematical tool for the job at hand:
$T_n = a + (n - 1)d$

Next, we substitute our known values into the formula. This step demonstrates to the examiner that you understand exactly how to map the theoretical construct onto the specific numerical parameters of the problem. Use brackets when substituting to prevent sign errors.
$T_{20} = 3 + (20 - 1)(4)$

At this stage, the algebra is done, and the problem reduces to arithmetic. However, you must respect the order of operations—BODMAS. Calculate the value inside the bracket first.
$T_{20} = 3 + (19)(4)$

Now we perform the multiplication. $19$ multiplied by $4$ is $76$. A common error here is to add the 3 to the 19 before multiplying by 4, which violates the fundamental rules of arithmetic. Multiplication always precedes addition.
$T_{20} = 3 + 76$

Finally, we perform the addition and arrive at the answer.
$T_{20} = 79$

State the final answer clearly. The 20th term is 79. This explicit conclusion earns the accuracy mark.

Precision and speed are essential in an exam setting. Notice the discipline in our approach: you identify the sequence type, you state the formula, you substitute the parameters correctly, and you calculate the final value step-by-step. There is no guessing, no ambiguity, and no room for structural errors.

## Subtopic: Exam Technique and Distinction-Level Takeaway

In the final examination, you must anticipate that questions will not always ask you to find a straightforward term as we just did. The examiners will test your agility with the formula. You may be given the value of $T_n$ and asked to find $n$, which is the number of terms or the position of a specific value.

The procedure remains identical. Do not invent new mathematics. You rely on the same fundamental tool. You state the formula $T_n = a + (n - 1)d$, you substitute the known values for $T_n$, $a$, and $d$, and then you solve the resulting linear equation for the unknown variable $n$.

When solving for $n$, remember the constraint we discussed at the very beginning of the lesson: $n$ must always be a strictly positive integer. It is a counting number. If you solve an exam equation for $n$ and you obtain a fraction, a decimal, or a negative number, you have either made an arithmetic calculation error, or the given value is simply not a term in that particular sequence. This constraint is a crucial, built-in diagnostic check during an exam. If $n = 4.5$, you know instantly that something is wrong. Stop, go back, and check your substitution and your signs.

The distinction-level takeaway is this: do not view the formula as a simple calculation tool, but rather as an interconnected system. Every arithmetic sequence problem is fundamentally a system defined by four variables: $a$, $d$, $n$, and $T_n$.

If you are given any three of these variables, you can always solve for the fourth using the general formula. Sometimes the examiners will hide $a$ and $d$ within simultaneous equations, giving you two different terms, such as $T_5 = 15$ and $T_10 = 30$. Even then, the underlying logic is exactly the same: construct the equations using $T_n = a + (n - 1)d$, and systematically solve for the missing pieces of the puzzle. Master this relationship, see the formula as a four-part system, and you will control the topic.
