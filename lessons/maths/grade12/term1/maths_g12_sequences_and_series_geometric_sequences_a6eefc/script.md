## Subtopic: Introduction and the General Formula

I am the Algebra Grandmaster. Today, we are focusing on geometric sequences. Precision is what earns you marks, so we start with the formula. A geometric sequence has a constant ratio between consecutive terms. You find this constant ratio, $r$, by taking any term and dividing it by the preceding term. For instance, $T_2$ divided by $T_1$, or $T_3$ divided by $T_2$.

The general formula for the $n$-th term of a geometric sequence is given by:
$T_n = a \cdot r^{n-1}$

Here, $a$ represents the first term, $T_1$. The variable $r$ is the constant ratio. And $n$ is the position of the term in the sequence. Ensure you understand this notation perfectly. The examiners will test your ability to apply this exact formula. Write it down immediately when you recognize a geometric sequence in your paper. That line earns the method mark for recalling the correct formula.

## Subtopic: Solving the Example Problem

Let us apply this directly. The examiner asks: Find the 10th term of the sequence 2; 6; 18; ...

Step one: identify the parameters. The first term, $a$, is clearly 2.
Step two: determine the constant ratio, $r$. We calculate $T_2$ over $T_1$, which is 6 divided by 2. That yields 3. We verify this by calculating $T_3$ over $T_2$, which is 18 divided by 6. That also yields 3. Our constant ratio $r$ is 3. The question asks for the 10th term, therefore $n = 10$.

Now, we substitute these values into our general formula, $T_n = a \cdot r^{n-1}$.
We substitute $n = 10$, $a = 2$, and $r = 3$.
This gives us: $T_{10} = 2 \cdot (3)^{10-1}$.
Simplify the exponent: $T_{10} = 2 \cdot (3)^9$.
Calculate $(3)^9$. You should use your calculator to find that $(3)^9$ is 19683.
Finally, multiply this by 2.
$T_{10} = 2 \cdot 19683 = 39366$.

That is your final answer. State it clearly. Every step is justified. Formula, substitution, calculation, result.

## Subtopic: Exam Techniques for Geometric Sequences

To top the class, you must think like the examiner. Where do students lose marks here? First, they confuse arithmetic and geometric sequences. Arithmetic relies on a common difference, found by subtraction. Geometric relies on a constant ratio, found by division. Always test the first three terms to confirm the sequence type.

Second, the placement of the exponent. The formula is $a \cdot r^{n-1}$. The exponent applies *only* to the ratio $r$, not to the product of $a$ and $r$. A common, fatal error is writing $(a \cdot r)^{n-1}$. Notice how I used parentheses for substitution: $2 \cdot (3)^{10-1}$. This enforces the correct order of operations.

In your exam, speed and precision are paramount. Write the formula. Extract your parameters $a$, $r$, and $n$. Substitute carefully using parentheses. Execute the calculation. That is the distinction-level approach.
