**Student**: Mandy, I don't get how he found the numbers 2 and 3 in the example. Where did they come from?

**Student**: Why do we split the middle term into two pieces instead of just doing something else?
**Mandy**: Good question! The goal is to turn our three-term trinomial into a four-term expression. Once it has four terms, we can split it down the middle and look at two pairs. That lets us pull out the common factors from each pair separately. It's like turning one big problem into two smaller ones that are easier to handle.

**Student**: What happens if I can't find two numbers that multiply to a*c and add to b?
**Mandy**: That’s a really important question! If you try all the factors and absolutely none of them work, it means the quadratic equation cannot be factored using this method. When that happens, you’ll have to use another tool, like the Quadratic Formula. But don't worry, for all the practice questions today, there will be two numbers that work!

**Student**: At the very end, he changed (x + 1) = 0 to x = -1. Why did the sign change?
**Mandy**: Because we are solving for x! If x + 1 equals 0, we need to get x all by itself. To move the +1 to the other side of the equals sign, we have to do the opposite math, which is subtracting 1. So, 0 minus 1 gives us -1.
