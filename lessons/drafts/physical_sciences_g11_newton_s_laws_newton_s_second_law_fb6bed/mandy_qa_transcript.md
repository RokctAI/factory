# Mandy Q&A Transcript - Newton's Second Law

**Student:** I'm a bit confused. The Grandmaster said the force was 20 N and that was the "net force." But aren't there other forces, like gravity?

**Mandy:** That is such a good question! Yes, gravity is definitely pulling down on the crate, and the surface is pushing up (we call that the normal force). But because the surface is completely flat and horizontal, the up and down forces perfectly cancel each other out. They balance! So, in the horizontal direction—the direction the crate is actually moving—the *only* force is that 20 N pull. Since it's the only one, it *is* the net force for that direction. Makes sense?

**Student:** Oh, okay! And what if there was friction? He said "frictionless."

**Mandy:** Bingo. If there was friction, it would fight against our pull. The net force wouldn't just be 20 N anymore. We'd have to take the 20 N pull and subtract whatever the friction force was to find the true, overall "net" force. But for this problem, they made it nice and simple for us to start!

**Student:** Why do we have to write the formula first? Can't I just put the numbers in?

**Mandy:** I totally get it, especially when the math feels easy. But writing the formula first is like drawing a map before a road trip. It proves to the person marking your paper that you know exactly *which* physics rule you're using. Plus, in harder problems later on, if you accidentally plug a number into the wrong spot, having the formula written down helps you catch your own mistake. It's a really good habit that will save you points in the exam!

**Student:** What does it mean that acceleration and mass are inversely proportional?

**Mandy:** Good catch on that vocabulary! "Inversely proportional" is just a fancy way of saying they do the opposite of each other. Think of pushing a shopping trolley. If it's empty (low mass), it's super easy to get it going fast (high acceleration). But if you fill it with a ton of groceries (high mass), and push with the exact same effort, it barely speeds up at all (low acceleration). When one goes up, the other goes down!
